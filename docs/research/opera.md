# Research dossier: the Paris Opéra (Palais Garnier) on a gas-lit performance night, March 1876

Scene id: `opera`. Prepared for the procedural cross-section illustrator. World units are metres.

Confidence conventions used throughout:

- **verified**: stated in a source listed in section 11 that was actually opened during this research. Page numbers "N p.xx" refer to Charles Nuitter, *Le Nouvel Opéra* (Hachette, 1875), written by the Opéra's own archivist in the year the house opened.
- **derived**: computed or measured from verified figures or from scaled historical drawings (method shown).
- **[illustrative]**: a reasonable reconstruction or artistic choice that the sources consulted do not confirm. Use it freely in the drawing, never as fact in a caption.

Where sources disagree, both figures are given and the disagreement is flagged.

## 1. Subject and date

**What we depict.** The Nouvel Opéra in Paris (today called the Palais Garnier), designed by Charles Garnier, on the evening of **Wednesday 8 March 1876**, during its first full season. That night the bill was Gounod's *Faust* (Chronopera performance database: "Mer. 08/03/1876 Faust (opéra)"). verified

In 1876 the company was officially the Théâtre national de l'Opéra (name used after 1870, en.wikipedia Paris Opera) and the façade bore the inscription "Académie Nationale de Musique" (Baedeker 1884). verified. Do not label anything "Palais Garnier" on canvas; use "the Opéra" or "the New Opéra".

**Why this moment.**

1. The building is complete and in daily use (inaugurated 5 January 1875; official Opéra history page and Wikipedia), yet still in its original state: Lenepveu's painted ceiling, gas lighting, hand-worked stage machinery, an iron *mesh* safety curtain, unfinished side pavilions. verified
2. It is superbly documented for exactly this date: Nuitter's 1875 guide gives the backstage arrival order of every trade, the gas, heating, water and electric installations, and long statistical tables; Garnier's own longitudinal section of the understage survives at the BnF; Baedeker's guides of 1878 and 1884 give the audience rules and prices; Chronopera gives the nightly bill.
3. Gas light is the soul of the subject. Full electric lighting came only in **1887**, installed by the Edison company with power generated on site (fr.wikipedia Opéra Garnier). Earlier electric trials are dated **1881** (en.wikipedia; also "partially electrified from 1881" for the chandelier, fr.wikipedia) or **1882** (grand foyer, then auditorium; fr.wikipedia). verified, sources disagree on the trial year. In March 1876 the only electric light in the building is a handful of arc-lamp effects fed by batteries (N p.229 to 232).
4. *Faust* is ideal for animation: a waltz crowd, a garden, a church scene with the organ, the soldiers' chorus, and a Walpurgis Night ballet in the last act. It came to the Opéra in 1869 with an added ballet and "became the most frequently performed opera at that house" (en.wikipedia Faust). verified

**Real example(s) the drawing is modelled on.** The real building on its central north-south axis, using: Nuitter 1875; Garnier's "Dessous de la scène, coupe longitudinale" (BnF Gallica btv1b53180096c, viewed as a Wikimedia Commons reproduction); the plans at ground floor and first-loge level (Mead 1991, Commons); transverse sections through the auditorium (Beauvert 1996) and the stage house (Steinhauser 1969). The *people* are fictional. The cast singing *Faust* that night is not recorded in sources consulted and must be invented.

**Optional variant.** A toggle to "after 1887" may switch the house to electric light (incandescent lamps in the same fittings; the chandelier then carried 320 bulbs, fr.wikipedia). Everything else in this dossier is for 1876.

**Real people (captions only, never characters):** Charles Garnier (architect), Charles Nuitter (archivist), Hyacinthe Halanzier (director 1871 to 1879), Louis Mérante (ballet master 1869 to 1887), Caroline "Madame Dominique" (dance school head 1870 to 1879), Jules Duboscq (optician in charge of electric light), Brabant (chief machinist), Paul Baudry, Jules-Eugène Lenepveu, Gustave Boulanger (painters), Marshal MacMahon (President, whose box was on the jardin side). Every person in section 4.4 is fictional.

## 3. Cutaway composition

### 3.1 Viewpoint and what is cut

A single longitudinal section on the north-south axis. **The viewer stands on the east side looking west**, so the façade and the Place are on the left and the administration on the right. The near (east) half is removed. Beyond the cut we see the west half: the far walls of the auditorium boxes, the west wings of the stage with their lattice counterweight towers, the unfinished Emperor's pavilion dome behind the auditorium, and the Foyer du Chant beyond the Foyer de la Danse.

Note: the Cavaillé-Coll organ (in the last two stage boxes on the spectators' right, N p.237) and the subscribers' carriage pavilion are on the east and therefore cut away. Show the organ in a close-up inset or a ghosted outline.

### 3.2 Left to right, bottom to top

Left to right: Place and perron, portico and vestibules under the loggia and Grand Foyer, the avant-foyer and the great staircase hall, the horseshoe auditorium with the Rotonde des abonnés beneath it, the proscenium and pit, the stage house (flies above, five understage levels and the flooded cistern below), the corridor, the Foyer de la Danse stacked over the extras' dressing room and the chorus hall, the costume stores and workshops above, then the administration and its walled courtyard.

Bottom to top: foundation wells and cistern (-18 to -6.5), cellars and understage (-6.5 to +8.5), the public floors (+0 to +32), attic stores (+30 to +38), the auditorium dome (to about +50 [illustrative]), the stage-house gable (+56) and Apollo (+63.5).

### 3.3 Aspect ratio

Building length about 173 m, height about 82 m (wells to Apollo). With a strip of Place on the left and courtyard on the right, use **about 2.4:1** (for example 2400 by 1000 world-pixel units, roughly 190 m by 80 m of world). If the frame must be shorter, crop the foundation wells; never crop Apollo.

### 3.4 Palette

- Exterior: blond Euville stone, coloured marbles, gilded bronze, gilded groups at the façade corners (fr.wikipedia). verified. Copper dome over the auditorium (fr.wikipedia); in 1876 it was only a few years old, so draw it **brown with green streaks** rather than full verdigris [illustrative].
- Auditorium: **red and gold** are the governing colours (N p.155). verified
- Grand Foyer: **old gold**, not bright gilt (N p.110). verified
- Staircase: white marble steps, honey onyx rails, dark red balusters, green bases (N p.81). verified (colours of the named marbles [illustrative])
- Backstage: raw wood, grey painted iron, hemp rope, canvas flats in distemper colours [illustrative].
- Gas light colour: warm yellow-orange; stage battens and footlights slightly whiter; "au bleu" pilot flames blue [illustrative].

**Audience by class.** Subscribers (abonnés) in premières loges, some of whom hold three nights a week and may visit the Foyer de la Danse; members of the Jockey Club (club rooms at 1 rue Scribe from 1863, en.wikipedia) who skip the first act. Gentlemen only in the orchestra stalls, evening dress obligatory there and in the first gallery (Baedeker 1884). Men in black tailcoats, white tie, carrying collapsible opera hats (gibus, en.wikipedia Opera hat); they keep hats with them and may wear them in the intervals (Baedeker). Ladies in the boxes in evening gowns of the early bustle period (1869 to 1876, en.wikipedia Bustle), bare shoulders, long gloves, fans, opera glasses. The pit: men, some women, shopkeepers and students; "women are seldom seen in the parterre, except at the Opera" (Baedeker 1878). The top tiers: students and music lovers with scores.

## 5. Animals and small life

- **Horses on stage**: grooms bring horses that "an elevator will hoist to the level of the stage" (N p.188). verified. Garnier's first scenery lift, worked by winches and counterweights, took **twenty minutes** to rise and was nicknamed the "monte-crottin" because horses and carts went up in it (fr.wikipedia). verified (secondary). *Faust* does not need horses; show one being hoisted by day for another production [illustrative].
- **Carriage horses** on the Place and in the subscribers' covered carriage entrance (N p.75). verified
- **Swallows** nest in the garlands of the façade frieze (N, dedication). verified. Lovely for a daytime view.
- **Fish in the cistern**: fish fed by staff are recorded "at one time" with no date (fr.wikipedia). Do not show them in 1876.
- **Rats, mice, cats, pigeons**: plausible in any 19th-century theatre and on its roofs, but not documented in sources consulted. [illustrative, keep small]
- **"Rats"** was slang for young ballet pupils (verse quoted in en.wikipedia Emma Livry). verified. A visual pun: a real mouse beside a pupil.
- **Beehives on the roof** are a modern addition. Do not draw them.

## 6. Machinery and moving parts

**General principle.** In 1876 everything is worked by hand. Garnier and a commission studied hydraulic presses that would lift the whole stage floor, but the 1870 war, the fire of the old house and cost (four million francs and two years' work) stopped it; the traditional "Italian" machinery was kept, rebuilt in iron (N p.168 to 173). verified. Electric motors arrived only after the First World War (fr.wikipedia). verified

| Mechanism | How it works | Animation speed |
|---|---|---|
| Chariots and masts (1st understage) | Iron carriages run on rails under slots ("costières"); a mast rises through the slot to hold a wing flat; a drum winds them on and off together (N p.163; fr.wikipedia) | glide 0.5 to 1 m/s [ill.] |

## 7. Environment

- **Season and sky**: early March, sunset about 17:54, dark by about 18:25 (derived). Weather that night is not recorded; use a cold, damp, partly cloudy evening [illustrative]. By day, chimney smoke from 14 furnaces and 450 fireplaces (N p.222) and from the rear chimneys whose stacks carried masks topped with coppered cast-iron crowns (fr.wikipedia).
- **Surroundings**: the new Place de l'Opéra, cleared by removing four to five hundred houses (Baedeker 1884); tall apartment blocks around. The Avenue de l'Opéra toward the Louvre is unfinished: in 1875 it stopped at rue Louis-le-Grand (N p.125) and was completed in 1879 (fr.wikipedia). Draw scaffolding and demolition beyond. No trees on the avenue: Garnier fought them (fr.wikipedia). verified
- **View from the stage roof**: Montmartre and Belleville heights, the Mont Valérien, the woods of Meudon, factory chimneys and moving locomotive smoke on the Western railway lines (N p.67). verified
- **Street life**: carriages arriving under the subscribers' pavilion; queues for the pit an hour before the doors open (Baedeker 1878); programme sellers; cab horses steaming [illustrative].
- **Ambient sound** (for audio):
  - Before curtain: carriage wheels and hooves on paving, door slams, the murmur of 2,000 people, orchestra tuning to the oboe's A [illustrative], hiss of thousands of gas jets [illustrative].
  - Backstage: the stage manager's bell (N p.182), electric bells installed throughout (N p.161), callers shouting names, creak of drums and blocks, rope running through sheaves, iron chariots rolling, hammering, the thump of flats set down, extras' boots.
  - Music of *Faust*: Act 2 waltz "Ainsi que la brise légère", the Act 4 soldiers' chorus "Gloire immortelle de nos aïeux", the church scene with organ, the Walpurgis Night ballet (fr.wikipedia Faust; en.wikipedia Faust). verified
  - Applause: the claque's sudden, synchronised clapping from the pit under the chandelier, "the obtrusive and simultaneous vigour of their exertions" (Baedeker 1878); cries of "bis"; fans and silk rustle.
  - Below: dripping water in the cistern, furnaces roaring, coal shovels [illustrative].
  - After midnight: footsteps of the fire patrol, a distant cab [illustrative].

18. **Ventilation (31).** "The chandelier is also a chimney: the heat of its flames pulls stale air up into an iron flue 8 metres wide that ends in the dome's lantern." Source: N p.228, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
19. **House lights (30).** "Gas cannot make a theatre dark. During the acts the house lights are only turned down 'to blue'." Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_Garnier . verified
20. **Pit (22).** "Under the chandelier sits the claque, paid applauders who enter by the stage door in groups of five, before the public, watched by their chief." Source: N p.185; Baedeker 1878, https://archive.org/details/parisanditsenvi00baedgoog . verified
21. **Stalls (23).** "No ladies in the orchestra stalls, and evening dress required: a stall costs 13 francs, a place in the pit 7." Source: Baedeker 1884, https://archive.org/details/parisenvironshand00karl ; Baedeker 1878. verified
22. **Subscribers (25).** "The ballet never comes in the first act: the gentlemen of the Jockey Club are still at dinner." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jockey-Club_de_Paris . verified
23. **Jeu d'orgue (35).** "The 'gas organ': a bank of pipes and taps under the stage front. Turning one graduated wheel brings day or night to any part of the stage." Source: N p.218 to 220, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
24. **Gas totals (35).** "Ten gas meters serve up to 9,200 jets through 25 kilometres of pipe and 714 taps." Source: N p.216 to 218, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
25. **Footlights (34).** "The footlights burn upside down, each flame drawn downward into a chimney, so a dancer's skirt can brush the glass. The dancer Emma Livry died after her skirt caught fire on a gaslight in 1862." Source: N p.221; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emma_Livry . verified
26. **Electric light (40, 55).** "Electric light here is still a stage effect: arc lamps fed by 70 Bunsen cells in a ground-floor laboratory, 122 metres from the far end of the stage." Source: N p.229 to 230, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
27. **Electric light, later (40).** "The whole building went electric only in 1887, when the Edison company wired it and generated power on site." Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_Garnier . verified (other sources date trials to 1881 or 1882)
28. **Stage (37).** "The stage is 26 metres deep and 53 wide, and slopes toward the audience by almost 5 centimetres every metre." Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_Garnier . verified
29. **Machinists (37).** "Seventy machinists work a normal night. The ship scene of L'Africaine needed forty extra carpenters underneath." Source: N p.184, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
30. **Drums (45).** "Wooden drums two metres across gather dozens of ropes, so a whole set can change before the audience's eyes in seconds." Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_Garnier . verified
31. **Rope (40, 41).** "223 kilometres of hemp rope, plus wire ropes twisted from 7,563 kilometres of iron wire." Source: N p.244, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
32. **Safety curtain (36).** "Between stage and audience hangs a curtain of iron mesh. A solid iron curtain replaced it only in 1896." Source: N p.160; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_Garnier . verified
33. **Cistern (46).** "Digging hit groundwater. Pumps ran day and night for eight months; then Garnier built a watertight concrete tank instead. It became the fire brigade's reservoir, and the 'lake' of legend." Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_Garnier . verified
34. **Cistern depth (46).** "The floor of the cistern lies 10.13 metres below the Place de l'Opéra." Source: N p.250, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
35. **Apollo (43).** "Apollo lifts his golden lyre 63 metres above the square. The bronze group weighs 13 tonnes and is also a lightning conductor." Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_Garnier ; N p.250. verified (height derived: 55.97 + 7.50 m)
36. **Foyer de la Danse (49).** "The dancers' warm-up room. Its floor slopes exactly like the stage and is never waxed. The mirror is in three pieces: Saint-Gobain had no table big enough to cast it whole." Source: N p.176 and p.181, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
37. **Foyer de la Danse (49).** "Subscribers with three nights a week may come here between acts to talk with the dancers, a custom dating from 1770." Source: N p.173, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
38. **Dancer's basket (49).** "In the basket: spare shoes, a shoehorn, gum, rice powder, a little bottle of water, rosin. Before her entrance she whitens her arms and crushes rosin under her shoe." Source: N p.182, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
39. **Extras (50).** "Most extras are workmen earning a second wage after their day's labour. When La Juive is on the bill they come in crowds." Source: N p.186, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
40. **Heating (6).** "Fourteen furnaces in the cellars can burn 10 tonnes of coal a day, and the offices and dressing rooms have 450 fireplaces." Source: N p.222, https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_HCY6AAAAIAAJ . verified
41. **Tonight (37).** "Wednesday 8 March 1876: Faust. The Opéra played on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, and in winter on Saturdays too." Source: http://chronopera.free.fr/ (search by date); Baedeker 1884, https://archive.org/details/parisenvironshand00karl . verified

## 9. Hidden details and stories

1. **The architect's signature** in the arabesques of the Rotonde vault. Documented (fr.wikipedia; N p.77).
2. **Garnier's face on Apollo**: two gilded busts near the Grand Foyer ceiling. Documented (fr.wikipedia).
3. **A star on a forehead**: a portable battery hidden in a performer's headdress makes a star twinkle (N p.232). Documented practice; put it on a fairy in the Walpurgis ballet [placement invented].
4. **Canes for swords**: at an uncostumed rehearsal the chorus brandish canes and umbrellas instead of blades; extras in work blouses, machinists in work clothes (N p.192). Documented. Show it in a daytime scene.
5. **The handkerchief claim**: a pit regular ties his handkerchief to the bench to keep his seat during the interval (Baedeker 1878). Documented.
6. **Smelling salts** at the contrôle for a fainting lady (fr.wikipedia). Documented practice.
7. **Roller-skating dancers**: pupils practise on roller skates in the corridors around the staircase dome for the skaters' ballet of *Le Prophète* (fr.wikipedia; roller skates used since 1849, en.wikipedia Le prophète). Documented (secondary source, date unspecified).
8. **Swallows' nests** in the façade garlands (N, dedication). Documented.
9. **A gentleman disguised as an extra**: at early performances of *L'Africaine*, music lovers unable to get seats enlisted as extras, betrayed by their fine white linen (N p.187). Documented anecdote, but from the old house; at Garnier this is plausible invention.
10. **The empty plinths**: marble sheaths in the corridors meant for busts; the Le Peletier busts burned in 1873, so a few survivors, plaster casts and lent vases stand in (N p.135 to 136). Documented.
11. **The curtained-off glacier and smoking room**: unfinished for lack of money, replaced by "small provisional recesses closed by hangings" (N p.128). Documented. Show a curtain with bare plaster behind.
12. **The fireman and the painter**: Baudry asked for a fire post near his studio; a corporal agreed a fire would be a disaster, because "oil paint makes very bad smoke" (N p.135). Documented anecdote (place a fireman sniffing the air near a painting as a nod).
13. **The Saint Bartholomew bell**: a bell said to have rung the signal for the 1572 massacre was used more than 500 times in *Les Huguenots*, then destroyed in the 1873 fire (N p.238); Nuitter calls the tradition unverifiable. The ten new bells hang in the flies; show an old machinist telling the story to the apprentice. Story documented as tradition; the scene invented.
14. **Musicians' cigarettes**: they toss the ends in the courtyard before going in (N p.188). Documented.
15. **A cat on the gril** watching a sparrow through the roof vents. Plausible invention.

## 10. Pitfalls

- **No Chagall ceiling** (installed 1964 over Lenepveu's, fr.wikipedia). Draw Lenepveu's sky-and-figures ceiling: sun over the stage, moon opposite, dawn and dusk at the sides (N p.142).
- **No electric house lighting** before 1887; only arc spots and limelight in 1876. No electric bulbs in the chandelier (fr.wikipedia; N p.229).
- **Gas cannot black out the house**: keep it dim, not dark.
- **No solid fire curtain**: mesh in 1876, solid from 1896 (fr.wikipedia; N p.160). The compulsory solid curtain followed the Opéra-Comique fire of 25 May 1887, which killed 84 (en.wikipedia Opéra-Comique; fr.wikipedia).
- **The chandelier never fell.** On 20 May 1896 a 750 kg counterweight crashed through the ceiling and killed a woman in the fourth tier during *Hellé*, not *Faust* (fr.wikipedia). That is 20 years after our date, and the "Phantom" novel dates from 1910. No phantom, no box 5 plaque (2011), no boat on the cistern.
- **The "lake" is a flooded brick cellar** about 1.3 m deep on average (derived), not a cavern. No boats, no fish in 1876.
- **No lifts for the public**: the Aga Khan's lift dates from 1920; staff lifts from the 1950s (fr.wikipedia).
- **Unfinished parts**: the Emperor's pavilion with rough-cut stones (fr.wikipedia), the glacier rotunda and smoking room curtained off (N p.128). The library-museum moved into the Emperor's pavilion later.
- **No imperial N and E** in the attic medallions (removed after 1870).
- **No Wagner**: *Lohengrin* entered the repertoire only in 1891 (official Opéra history).
- **Degas**: many famous rehearsal pictures show the old Le Peletier house (for example *Le Foyer de la danse à l'Opéra de la rue Le Peletier*, about 1872, Musée d'Orsay, per fr.wikipedia). Borrow costumes and poses, not rooms.
- **Ballet skirts**: knee-to-calf tulle skirts and fitted bodices, not 20th-century flat tutus (Degas painting description, fr.wikipedia; official history on Romantic tutus).
- **Women's fashion**: early bustle with draped overskirts (1869 to 1876, en.wikipedia Bustle), not the huge 1885 shelf bustle and not crinolines.
- **Firemen** belong to a military regiment (1866 to 1967), not the modern Brigade (fr.wikipedia BSPP).
- **No hydraulic stage, no motors**: hand power, ropes and counterweights only (N p.172; fr.wikipedia).
- **Conductor's place**: until late in the century the conductor beat time with his back to the orchestra (fr.wikipedia Orchestre, single secondary source), not facing the players in the modern way.
- **The avenue is not finished** (1879) and has no trees.
- **Copper roofs are not yet bright green** [illustrative caution].
- **No women in the orchestra stalls** (Baedeker 1884).
- **Scene-painting shops** were not in the building; the Ateliers Berthier came later (fr.wikipedia). Scenery arrives by cart and the scenery lift.
- **Names**: "Palais Garnier" is later usage; in 1876 it is the New Opéra, the Théâtre national de l'Opéra.
