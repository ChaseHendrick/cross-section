# Research dossier: Salisbury Cathedral under construction, midsummer 1245

Scene id: `cathedral`. Prepared for the procedural cross-section illustrator. World units are metres.

Confidence conventions used throughout:

- **verified**: stated in a source listed in section 11 that was actually opened during this research.
- **derived**: computed from verified figures (the arithmetic is shown).
- **[illustrative]**: a reasonable reconstruction or artistic choice that the sources consulted do not confirm. Use it freely in the drawing, but never present it as fact in captions.

Where two sources disagree, both are given and the disagreement is flagged.

## 1. Subject and date

**What we depict.** The Cathedral Church of the Blessed Virgin Mary at Salisbury (New Sarum), Wiltshire, England: a real building, shown on **a working weekday in late June 1245** (Julian calendar). The finished east end (Trinity Chapel, quire, eastern transepts, presbytery) is in daily use for services, the main (western) transepts are complete, and the ten-bay nave is rising to the west, with its western bays still low and its eastern bays reaching roof level.

**Why this moment is defensible.**

- Foundation stones laid **28 April 1220**; by **1225** the east end "had progressed far enough for three altars to be consecrated" (VCH Wilts vol. 3; Salisbury Cathedral website). verified
- Quire and eastern transept completed **1234 to 1236**, the choir stalls made about **1236**, and "shortly after" the main transept; under Bishop Robert Bingham the quire roof (and possibly the main transept roof) was covered with lead and glazed; "in the 1240s the nave was built"; the nave was "completed 1245 to 1250"; the whole church was consecrated in **1258** with Henry III present, and the nave vault was possibly put up only after that (German Wikipedia, citing Clifton-Taylor and Ute Engel). verified (secondary source)
- "By 1258 the nave, transepts and choir were complete" and the main body took "some 38 years" (English Wikipedia). verified
- The exact state of each nave bay in June 1245 is not recorded anywhere consulted. The bay-by-bay staging in section 3 is an **[illustrative]** reconstruction consistent with the dates above.

**Why Salisbury and not Amiens, Reims, Chartres or Wells.**

1. It was built almost entirely in one campaign (1220 to 1258), so it "has by far the most consistent architectural style of any medieval English cathedral" (Wikipedia). One coherent design makes a clean cutaway.
2. Its building history is documented in English county history (VCH Wiltshire), with named officials, funding methods and dates.
3. It suits "east end in use, nave still rising" perfectly, because it was built strictly east to west.
4. It has no crypt to complicate the section: it stands on foundations only 1.2 m deep over wet gravel (see section 2). The brief asks for a crypt "if the example has one"; Salisbury does not (no source consulted mentions one, and the shallow foundations on a high water table make one impossible to draw honestly).
5. Amiens Cathedral was begun in the same year, 1220 (German Wikipedia), which gives a nice comparison caption, but its documentation in English is thinner.

**Named real people (captions only, never characters).** Bishop Richard Poore (moved the cathedral, bishop until 1228), Bishop Robert de Bingham (bishop 1229 to 1246, in office in 1245), Elias of Dereham (keeper of the fabric; died 1245 according to Matthew Paris), Master Nicholas of Ely (resident master mason, given a house plot c. 1230), William Longespée and Ela, Countess of Salisbury (laid foundation stones), King Henry III. Every character in the casting list (section 4.4) is fictional.

Note: "Nicholas of Ely" the master mason (VCH) is a different man from Nicholas of Ely the bishop and Lord Chancellor (died 1280) who has a Wikipedia article. Do not conflate them.

## 3. Cutaway composition

### 3.1 Viewpoint and what is cut

- One long longitudinal section along the main east-west axis. **The south half is cut away; the viewer stands south, looking north.** West (the building site) is on the left, the finished, consecrated east end on the right.
- Viewing from the south is deliberate: the cloisters and chapter house, which lie south and were begun only in the 1260s (VCH; dates vary by source), are in the removed half, so their absence is never an issue.
- Visible behind the cut: the inner face of the north arcade, north aisle and its lancets, the north transept (seen through the crossing arches), the north porch opening at mid-nave (EB1911 mentions "a lofty north porch"), and beyond the roofs the detached belfry and Old Sarum hill on the horizon (both genuinely lie north).
- The cut passes through: floors, foundations, vaults (shown as thin curved shells), roof trusses and lead coverings, scaffolds and cranes.

### 3.2 Left to right, bottom to top

Left to right: works yard (lodge, smithy, stone stacks, lime, ox carts) to the west; then the west-front footings; nave bays rising like a staircase from shin-high walls (bay 1) to full height with roof trusses and a great wheel (bays 9 and 10); a temporary timber screen at the west crossing; the crossing; the busy, candlelit quire with canons and vicars singing; the presbytery and high altar; the low, forest-like Trinity Chapel with its hair-thin dark shafts; beyond the east wall the masons' cottages and the new town.

Bottom to top: wet gravel and groundwater; 1.2 m footings; floor (beaten earth and stone chips in the nave, finished paving in the east [illustrative]); arcades; low triforium; clerestory; vaults (present in the east, absent in the nave); roof timbers and lead; sky with swifts.

### 3.3 Aspect ratio and framing

- World window x from -45 to 165 (210 m), y from -5 to 62 (67 m including the belfry spire in the background). Aspect about **3.1 : 1**; if the belfry is cropped to y = 48, about **4 : 1**.
- The centre of interest (x 40 to 75: the high nave bays, crane and crossing) should sit slightly left of centre.

## 4. People

### 4.1 Head-counts

No building accounts for Salisbury in the 1240s were found in the sources consulted. The nearest documented English figures:

- **Westminster Abbey, begun 6 July 1245**: "up to 400 workers on the site at a time" in summer, comprising "stonecutters, marblers, stone-layers, carpenters, painters and their assistants, marble polishers, smiths, glaziers, plumbers, and general labourers" (Wikipedia, Westminster Abbey). verified
- **Vale Royal Abbey, 1278 to 1280**: on average each month "135 men: 40 masons, 4 carpenters, 15 quarrymen, 36 diggers, 7 blacksmiths and 33 carters" (Wikipedia, Vale Royal Abbey, citing Knoop and Jones 1933). verified
- **Salisbury**: "Both skilled and unskilled workers" including "Women and children" who helped by "carrying goods, tending animals, and providing food" (Salisbury Cathedral website). verified
- **Clergy**: "52 or 53" prebends (canons) by the mid-13th century; residence was a recurring problem (in 1222 the minimum annual residence was cut to 40 days for three years); "a list of names of 52 vicars choral at Salisbury survives from 1297" (VCH vol. 3). verified

Suggested on-canvas population at midday (illustrative, scaled from the above): about 110 workers, 25 to 35 clergy and choirboys, and 10 to 15 others (sellers, children, visitors, a friar).

### 4.2 Roles (32 roles)

Clothing basis (verified, Wikipedia, 1200 to 1300 in European fashion): working men wore a short belted tunic slit up the front, corners tucked into the belt, long braies or leggings, sometimes hose; hats were a round brimmed cap, a beret-like cap, the **coif** ("a little tight white hood with strings that tied under the chin"), a straw hat, or the **chaperon** hood over the shoulders. "Apart from aprons for trades like smithing ... special clothes were not worn for working." Women wore long tunics to the ankle, linen headdresses, wimples and veils, or a barbette (chin band) with a coif. Wool was the main material; men's hair was moderate length, curled under at the neck.

## 6. Machinery and moving parts

### 6.1 Lifting

**Great wheel on the tie beams (zone 18).** Verified mechanics (Wikipedia, Treadwheel crane): a large wooden wheel turning on a central shaft "with a treadway wide enough for two workers walking side by side"; cranes on building sites were not set on scaffolds or thin walls but began "on the ground, often within the building", then were "dismantled and reassembled on the roof beams from where it was moved from bay to bay during construction of the vaults". They were "primarily capable of a vertical lift"; the crane master steered loads "by a small rope attached to the load"; they "rarely featured ratchets or brakes" because wheel friction stopped run-back. Blocks were lifted "by sling, lewis hole or devil's clamp", other loads in "baskets, wooden boxes or barrels".

**Important caveat (verified):** the earliest treadwheel reference is "in France about 1225", a manuscript image of about 1240, but "in England the treadwheel is not recorded before 1331". Spoked windlasses and crank windlasses were also in use. Recommended rendering: a large wheel on the nave tie beams that can be read as a treadwheel, with an on-canvas note that it is a plausible guess; or a spoked windlass hauled by hand. The large windlass wheel that survives inside Salisbury's tower (cathedral website) stands in the tower raised after 1300, so it cannot have worked there in 1245 (inference).

Animation numbers (all [illustrative], derived): wheel diameter 4.5 m (the Harwich harbour wheels are 4.88 m, verified, so this is plausible); axle drum 0.45 m; ratio about 10 : 1. A man walking the tread at 0.5 m/s turns the wheel at about 2.1 rpm and lifts the load at about 5 cm/s (3 m per minute), so a block from floor to wall plate (27 m) takes about 9 minutes. The wheel creaks; the rope pays in onto the drum in tight turns.

**Ground windlass (zone 12).** Horizontal drum on two A-frames with four radiating hand-spikes, two men; rope over a pulley on a timber gin pole lashed to the scaffold; drum about 0.3 m [illustrative]; lift speed similar.

**Lewis and tongs.** A lewis is set in a prepared hole in the top of a block; scissor-tongs (an "external lewis") grip the sides; "Medieval builders used external lewis variations suspended from cranes" (Wikipedia, Lewis, verified). Animate the tongs closing as the rope takes the strain.

**Ladders, hods, handbarrows, wheelbarrows.** Old and new methods "continued to coexist" (Wikipedia, Treadwheel crane, verified). Wheelbarrow: wheel at the front, first archival reference 1222 (Wikipedia, Wheelbarrow, verified).

### 6.2 Falsework and scaffolding

- **Centring**: timber frames supporting arch stones until the keystone is in, then "striking the centring" (Wikipedia, Centring, verified). Carpenters build centring "on the ground" and hoist it into place (Wikipedia, Flying buttress, verified). Animate: frame rising on ropes; later, mallet knocks out paired wedges and the frame drops a few centimetres.
- **Putlog scaffold**: putlogs in holes in the wall, either supported outside by standards or cantilevered; exterior holes filled when the scaffold is removed (Wikipedia, Putlog hole, verified). Poles lashed with rope, hurdle (woven wattle) platforms [lashing and hurdles illustrative]. Lifts about every 1.5 m [illustrative]. Platforms sway slightly when men walk on them.

### 6.3 Workshops

- **Smith's bellows**: paired leather bellows, about 20 strokes per minute [illustrative]; hearth glow pulses with each stroke; sparks on hammer blows (about 1 per second).
- **Pit saw**: two-man saw, stroke about 0.6 m, roughly 1 stroke per second [illustrative]; sawdust falls on the bottom sawyer.
- **Bankers**: mallet on chisel, 1.5 to 2 blows per second [illustrative], puffs of white dust, chips falling.
- **Lime**: quicklime made by heating limestone above about 840 deg C, usually about 900 to 1000 deg C, then "slaked lime" formed by mixing with water (Wikipedia, Lime kiln, verified). Non-hydraulic lime mortar sets slowly by carbonation in air (Wikipedia, Lime mortar, verified). Animate a steaming, bubbling slaking pit and hoes moving.
- **Lead casting**: lead melts at 327.5 deg C (Wikipedia, Lead, verified). Pot on a fire, ladle pour onto a sand bed, a board drawn along to level it [technique illustrative]; cooling sheet changes from bright mirror to dull grey over a minute or two [illustrative].
- **Glass**: full-size cartoon on a whitewashed table; pieces cut, edges "grozed"; black glass paint of powdered glass, iron filings and a binder, fired in a kiln; lead cames hold the pieces (Wikipedia, Stained glass, verified). Silver-stain yellow came only "soon after 1300" (verified): do not animate yellow staining.
- **Bell casting**: bells cast mouth down in a two-part mould, core and cope, shaped with strickle boards; principle unchanged "since the 12th century"; often "cast on site in pits in a church or its grounds" (Wikipedia, Bellfounding, verified). Bell metal is about 78 % copper, 22 % tin (Wikipedia, Bell metal, verified). Animate: furnace glow, pour of orange metal, smoke and steam from the pit.

### 6.4 Other motion

- **Ox carts**: about 3 km/h [illustrative]; wheels rotate slowly, oxen nod. The Vale Royal accounts record 35,000 cartloads of stone in 1277 to 1281, "over 30 per day", over nine miles of rough road (Wikipedia, Vale Royal Abbey, verified), which suggests a steady stream of carts.
- **Smoke**: from the smithy, the plumbers' fire, the bell furnace, and cottage roofs, drifting with the wind.
- **Candles and incense**: flames flicker in the quire; a thurible swings during High Mass (period about 1.5 s) [illustrative].
- **Bells**: the belfry bells swing for services [illustrative]; handbells in the procession.
- **Water**: the Avon flows beyond the east end; the Nadder joins the Avon near the cathedral (Wikipedia, River Nadder, verified); mill wheels and weirs on the Nadder (Wikipedia, Salisbury, verified) can turn slowly in the far distance.
- **Banners and cloth**: altar frontals stir; workmen's hoods flap on the roof.

## 7. Environment

- **Site**: a flat meadow called Myrifeld ("marshy ground", VCH vol. 6, verified), about 46 m above sea level on gravel at the meeting of the Avon, Nadder and Bourne (VCH vol. 6, verified). The Avon encircles the early city to the west and south. Show lush, wet green meadow with rushes, standing puddles and willows [vegetation illustrative].
- **North horizon (behind the building)**: Old Sarum, a hill fortress about 3 km north, with its castle and the old cathedral being dismantled for stone (Wikipedia, verified). Beyond, the open chalk downs of Salisbury Plain.
- **Town**: the new city laid out on a grid; plots of about 7 by 3 perches from 1225 (VCH vol. 6, verified); city charter 1227 (Wikipedia, verified). In 1245 timber houses, many still new.
- **Harnham Bridge (off-canvas south)**: built of stone by Bishop Bingham in 1244 "to protect traffic from the constant inundations of the river", with a chapel of St John the Baptist on its island (VCH vol. 6, verified).
- **Weather**: a bright, breezy midsummer day with high cumulus, occasional shower clouds in the west [illustrative]. Optional variants: a rain shower (masons retreat to the lodge, plumbers cover the fire), and a winter scene (see pitfalls: winter means covered walls and reduced wages).
- **Light** (derived from the geometry, viewer facing north): at midsummer the sun rises in the north-east, behind and to the right of the building; at midday it is high in the south, behind the viewer, lighting the cut face; it sets in the north-west, behind and to the left, back-lighting the west works with long golden light.

**Ambient sounds** (for optional audio, all [illustrative] in mix): chink of chisels and the dull knock of wooden mallets; rasp of the pit saw; adze thuds; the creak and groan of the great wheel and ropes; shouted commands; ox lowing and cart wheels grinding on gravel; anvil rings and bellows wheeze; hiss of water on hot iron and of slaking lime; plainchant drifting from the quire behind the screen, swelling when the screen door opens; belfry bells for the hours; swifts screaming, jackdaws, sparrows; river water and wind in the meadow; children shouting; the crack of cooling lead.

Additional fact cards (longer text, optional):

- **Westminster, the rival site.** Henry III began rebuilding Westminster Abbey on 6 July 1245; up to 400 workers were on site in summer (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey). verified
- **Winter on the site.** At Vale Royal, "during the winter months open stonework was covered with bundled hay to protect it from the elements" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vale_Royal_Abbey). verified (analogous site)
- **Fixed wages, 1351.** After the Black Death a statute fixed daily pay "as they were wont": master carpenter 3d, other carpenters 2d, master mason 4d, other masons 3d, their servants 1 1/2 d (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Labourers_1351). verified (later evidence; use only as a comparison)
- **Danger aloft.** At Canterbury the French master builder William of Sens fell fifty feet from the scaffolding and was seriously injured (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_cathedrals_and_churches). verified (different cathedral, 1170s)
- **A mason's notebook.** Villard de Honnecourt's portfolio of about 1225 to 1235 contains about 250 drawings, including buildings, lifting devices and a water-driven saw (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villard_de_Honnecourt). verified

## 10. Pitfalls

1. **No spire and no tall tower.** The tower was heightened and the spire built about 1300 to 1330. In 1245 show at most a low crossing stage just above the roofs [its exact 1245 form is unknown].
2. **No cloister, no chapter house.** Dates differ by source (Wikipedia says cloisters 1240 and chapter house 1263; VCH says both begun between 1263 and 1271; German Wikipedia gives cloister about 1270 to 1310 and chapter house 1263 to 1284). The south-side cutaway avoids them; do not draw them.
3. **No west front statues.** The west front was finished in 1265 and most of its present statues are 19th-century. In 1245 it is footings at most.
4. **Treadwheel caution.** No English record before 1331. If drawn, label it as a reconstruction. **No slewing jib cranes**: slewing cranes appear "as early as 1340" (Wikipedia, Treadwheel crane).
5. **Cranes do not sit on scaffolds or on thin walls.**
6. **No yellow silver-stain glass** (introduced soon after 1300). No tracery windows: Salisbury is lancet Gothic (simple plate tracery only in some transept and porch windows, German Wikipedia).
7. **Do not cover the nave in open flying buttresses.** German Wikipedia says the strong vertical accents of flyers belong to the later crossing tower (1320s, about 1415) and that George Gilbert Scott inserted flyers in the 19th century. Whether the 13th-century nave has buttressing hidden under the aisle roofs is not confirmed in the sources consulted [unverified].
8. **No apse.** The east end is square, with a low projecting Trinity Chapel.
9. **No crypt.** Foundations are only 1.2 m deep on wet gravel.
10. **No shrine of St Osmund and no pilgrim crowds.** Osmund was canonised only in 1457 (Wikipedia, Old Sarum Cathedral). Sources conflict on when his remains reached the new cathedral (1226 per German Wikipedia, 1457 per English Wikipedia's Old Sarum Cathedral article); either way there was no saint's shrine in 1245.
11. **No mechanical clock.** The cathedral clock dates from about 1386.
12. **No Magna Carta display case.** The cathedral's copy is connected to Elias of Dereham, but where it was kept in 1245 is not known; do not show it.
13. **No pews or chairs in the nave.** Modern seating (1,900 places, German Wikipedia) is modern. People stand or kneel.
14. **No Victorian fittings**: no Scott screen, no Willis organ, no William Pye font (2008), no gas lighting.
15. **Clothing**: no leather aprons for masons as a rule (the period source says special work clothes were not worn apart from aprons for trades like smithing); no hard hats, no trousers, no buttons down coats; women in wimples, veils or barbettes.
16. **Money**: only silver pennies (plus a few halfpennies and farthings struck in 1222). No gold coins until Henry III's gold penny of 1257; no groats until 1279 (Wikipedia, Penny).
17. **No spectacles**: the first eyeglasses were developed in northern Italy in the second half of the 13th century (Wikipedia, Glasses).
18. **Real people are not characters.** Elias of Dereham died in 1245 (Matthew Paris via Wikipedia); German Wikipedia says he served "until 1247": the sources conflict, which is another reason never to show him as a character.
19. **Master mason's name**: do not confuse Master Nicholas of Ely (mason) with Nicholas of Ely (bishop, died 1280).
20. **Do not draw regular, channelled "floated" water meadows** around the Close: the meadows of 1245 are marshy flood meadow. (The later date of the floated water-meadow system was not confirmed in the sources consulted [unverified]; plain marsh is the safe choice.)
21. **No peregrine falcons as a feature**: they are a modern story at the cathedral.
22. **No brick.** Everything is stone, timber, lead, glass, lime and thatch.
23. **Interior colour**: do not render a modern bare-stone look everywhere. Medieval interiors were "elaborate and brightly coloured" (BBC History); show limewash and painted lines in the finished east end [specific scheme illustrative].
24. **Foundation depth conflict**: use 1.2 m (cathedral website); ignore the 18 in figure in one Wikipedia article.

### Open questions

1. The exact state of each nave bay in June 1245 is not documented; the staircase staging is a reconstruction.
2. Whether Salisbury used a treadwheel or only windlasses in the 1240s (no English treadwheel record before 1331).
3. Precise heights of the arcade, triforium, clerestory, aisle vaults, Trinity Chapel and roof ridge were not found; only the 25.5 m high vault is verified.
4. The 13th-century form of the crossing tower before 1300, and whether the detached belfry was complete by 1245.
5. Where the masons' lodge, tracing floor and workshops actually stood (only "masons living just east of the Close" is recorded).
6. Salisbury's own 13th-century wage rates and workforce size (none found; Westminster 1245 and Vale Royal 1278 used as analogues).
7. Whether the quire vault paintings existed by 1245.
8. The exact Sarum daily timetable at Salisbury in 1245 (general medieval canonical-hour times used).
