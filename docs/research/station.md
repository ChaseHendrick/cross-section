# Research dossier: the International Space Station, May 2011 (scene id `station`)

Prepared for the scene illustrator. World units are metres. Every claim is tied to a source key (S-numbers, full URLs in section 11) or is flagged **[unverified, illustrative]**. Values marked **(derived)** are my own arithmetic from verified inputs. Where sources disagree, both values are given.

---

## 1. Subject and date

**Subject:** the International Space Station (ISS) as a real, single, well documented configuration: **Sunday 22 May 2011, GMT**, Flight Day 7 of Space Shuttle Endeavour's mission STS-134, during Expedition 27. This is not a composite: every module and every docked vehicle below was really attached on that day.

**Why this moment:**
- **Assembly essentially complete.** ESA calls AMS-02, delivered by STS-134 in May 2011, "the last major part of the Space Station delivered by a Space Shuttle" [S23]. Tranquility and the Cupola (February 2010) [S2, S21], Rassvet (May 2010) [S2] and the Leonardo storeroom (1 March 2011) [S11] are all in place.
- **The most people and vehicles at once.** After the hatches opened on 18 May, six station crew and six shuttle crew were aboard, twelve in all [S15]. NASA's rules for this kind of dual-docked operation allowed no more than 13 aboard [S17].
- **A photograph exists of exactly this configuration.** On 23 May 2011 the departing Soyuz TMA-20 backed off and Expedition 27's ESA flight engineer photographed the station with Endeavour docked; NASA calls these "the first taken of a shuttle docked to the International Space Station from the perspective of a Russian Soyuz spacecraft" [S3, S7]. The illustrator can treat that image set as the master reference.
- **A spacewalk day.** On 22 May two shuttle crew made the mission's second spacewalk, 8 hours 7 minutes long [S6], after sleeping overnight in the Quest airlock at reduced pressure [S16]. That puts the airlock, the spacesuits and people outside the hull into the picture on a documented timetable.

**Vehicles docked on 22 May 2011 (all verified):**

The layout of the drawing is a declared two-view arrangement (section 3) because the real station is cross-shaped.

## 3. Cutaway composition

### 3a. The problem

The station is a cross, not a ship. The pressurised "spine" (Zvezda, Zarya, Unity, Destiny, Harmony) runs fore and aft along the direction of travel, while the 108.5 m truss runs across it, mounted on top of Destiny (S0 "was mounted on top of the U.S. Lab Destiny" [S2]). Kibo and Columbus stick out sideways from Harmony; Tranquility (with the Cupola under it) and Quest stick out sideways from Unity [S2]. No single flat section shows both the truss and the spine. Any one-plane cut either hides the truss end-on or hides the Russian segment behind Unity.

### 3b. Recommended layout: a two-view "double-page spread" in one panorama

Draw one wide canvas holding **two true orthographic views side by side**, sharing the same vertical scale and the same Earth below, like the left and right pages of a cross-section book. Label the join with a curved arrow and an axis glyph: "The truss crosses the module line at right angles above Destiny; here it is turned to face you."

- **Panel A, "Along the station" (left, x 0 to 80):** looking at the station's **port side**, so **forward (Endeavour) is on the left and Zvezda and ATV-2 on the right**. Nadir (Earth) is down, zenith up. Cut away the near (port) half of every module along the spine. Everything attached above or below the spine is in its true place: Endeavour, Harmony, Destiny with the S0 truss on its roof, Unity with Z1 above and Leonardo below, Zarya with Rassvet and Soyuz TMA-20 below, Zvezda with Poisk and Soyuz TMA-21 above and Pirs and Progress below, ATV-2 behind.
- **Panel B, "Across the station" (right, x 95 to 212):** looking **forward from behind the station** (the wake view), so **port (P6) is on the left and starboard (S6) on the right**. The full truss runs across the top with its eight solar wings and radiators. Below it, two transverse "slices" of the station are pulled apart vertically (the exploded effect): the **Harmony slice** (Kibo with its porch and logistics module, Harmony in cross-section, Columbus) and, lower down, the **Unity slice** (PMA-3, Tranquility with the Cupola under it, Unity in cross-section, Quest). Draw Harmony and Unity in Panel B only as ghosted hubs; their people live in Panel A.

**Coordinates.** World units are metres. In both panels **y = 0 is the axis of the main module line**; positive y is zenith (away from Earth), negative is toward Earth. Add a constant (for example +45) if the engine needs y to be positive.
- Panel A: x = 12 + s, where s is metres aft of PMA-2's forward face.
- Panel B: x = 155 + Y, where Y is metres to starboard of the station centreline. The truss keeps its true height (y 3.0 to 7.6). The Harmony slice is lowered so its axis is at y = -8, the Unity slice to y = -24 (exploded positions; their true axes are at y = 0).

**Depth.** Use the engine's oblique depth (z) only for things on the far side of each cut: in Panel A, the starboard truss and starboard solar wings recede up-right behind S0; in Panel B, the spine modules recede behind the hubs.

**Suggested canvas:** x from -2 to 214, y from -46 to +46, about **2.4 : 1**. Black sky above, a curved Earth limb filling the bottom fifth.

**Which way it flies.** With a shuttle docked, "the -XVV +ZLV attitude is the standard attitude for the Orbiter-ISS stack" [S17]: the station flew yawed round, Zvezda's end first. In Panel A, the station therefore moves to the **right** and the Earth below drifts left. Endeavour's "nose pointed toward deep space and its payload bay toward the station" [S15].

**How to show weightlessness:**
- No chairs, no "lying down" (there is "no real up or down" [S32]). US modules still have a conventional "deck" (Earth side) and "overhead", so most people float roughly upright but at varied angles; one or two should be sideways or upside down.
- People move by gliding between handholds and are held at worksites by foot restraints and handrails (Cupola fittings include "handrails, handholds, tethers" and a "foot restraint device" [S21]; crews "glide from one module to another" [S35]). Glide speed about 0.3 to 0.6 m/s **[unverified, illustrative]**.
- Loose items have Velcro or bungees: every food packet has "a little Velcro dot" [S34]. Long hair spreads in a cloud, cables and straps drift in slow curves, drops of water are spheres ("Catch any water drops or stray food" [S34]).
- Sleepers hang in bags fixed "to a wall or the ceiling" [S25], near an air vent [S32].
- Exercise needs straps: the treadmill harness and bungees pull the runner down [S36]; on the bicycle "No seat required!" [S36].

**Light.** Interior lighting in 2011 was fluorescent; NASA was still preparing to replace it with LEDs in 2014 [S47]. Use even, cool-white panel light, dimmed in crew quarters at night. Outside, the Sun rises and sets every ~45 minutes (16 of each per day [S1]): animate the orbital day and night on a fast ~91.5-minute cycle independent of the GMT crew clock (section 7).

### 3d. Exploded slices

## 5. Animals and small life

- **No animals should be drawn as pets or stowaways.** No cats, rats, dogs or birds have any place on the station. I found **no source** for animals aboard on 22 May 2011. Small creatures carried on STS-134 as experiments (spiders, tardigrades) are sometimes mentioned in secondary accounts but I could **not verify** them; leave them out unless confirmed.
- **Plants:** the Russian Lada greenhouse "has been in almost continuous use" since 2002, growing "sweet peas, wheat, tomatoes, and lettuce" [S2]. A small box of green seedlings under a lamp is justified; what was growing on 22 May is **[unverified]**.
- **Microbes:** crews sample air and surfaces with a microbial air sampler and surface kits [S2]. Draw a crew member pressing a sample slide to a panel.
- **Lab biology:** Columbus's BioLab handles "microorganisms, cells, tissue cultures, small plants, and small invertebrates", with centrifuges and a microscope [S2]. Show sealed containers, not visible animals.
- **Toy "zero-g indicator":** crews carry small toys that start floating when they reach weightlessness; a published list documents many, including Soyuz TMA-22 later in 2011, but none for TMA-20 or TMA-21 [S14]. If used, draw a generic plush toy on a string in a Soyuz descent module **[practice documented, toy for these flights unverified]**.

## 7. Environment

**Sky and Earth.** The station is about 354 km up [S3]. Below, Earth fills the lower view as a curved limb with a thin blue atmosphere edge **[illustrative]**: ocean, cloud bands, deserts and, at night, city lights. Crews photograph "oil spills, desertification, melting icebergs and glaciers, dust storms, hurricanes ... and auroras" from the Cupola [S22]. The 51.6-degree orbit [S2] means the ground track never goes further north or south than 51.6 degrees latitude **(derived)**.

**Day and night.** 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every 24 hours [S1]; about 55 to 60 min of sunlight and up to about 36 min of darkness per orbit **(derived)**. Exterior lighting changes "from direct sunlight to earthshine to total darkness" quickly [S47]. Drive the outside light from a separate orbital phase: phase = (GMT minutes / 91.5) mod 1. Keep the crew's working day on the GMT clock [S32].

**Cabin conditions.** One atmosphere, shirt-sleeve environment, 18.3 to 26.7 C, humidity 25 to 70% (Kibo specification) [S27].

**Ambient sounds (optional audio).**
- Constant fan and pump hum: background levels "measured 56-69 dBA" [S46].
- Treadmill running: "77 dBA" [S46]; ARED clanks **[illustrative]**.
- Alarms must be 20 dBA above the background [S46].
- Radio: Daily Planning Conference calls with the ground [S19]; the shuttle crew's wake-up music [S16].
- Sleepers wear earplugs [S32].
- **Outside: silence.** Inside a spacesuit, only breathing and the suit's fan **[illustrative]**.

**Optional ground inset.** A strip along the bottom edge showing small control rooms (Houston, Korolev, Oberpfaffenhofen, Tsukuba, Huntsville, Toulouse, Saint-Hubert [S2]), with the G-cast at consoles.

## 10. Pitfalls

- **Wrong era modules.** Do not draw Nauka (arrived 2021 on Zvezda's Earth-facing port [S23], where Pirs sat in 2011 [S2]), BEAM, Prichal, docking adapters on PMA-2 or the newer roll-out solar arrays (all later than 2011 **[dates unverified here]**). Pirs **is** present in 2011 [S2].
- **Wrong vehicles.** No Dragon or Cygnus in 2011 (still future in the 2010 guide [S2]); no HTV on 22 May (the table in section 1 is complete for that day).
- **Wrong places.** Leonardo is on Unity's Earth-facing port in 2011 (moved to Tranquility only in 2015 [S11]). PMA-3 is on the port end of Tranquility [S2]. The Cupola is on Tranquility's Earth-facing side [S2]; ESA's specification page mentions a forward port, which describes a different arrangement, so follow NASA's guide.
- **The truss crosses the modules at right angles** [S2]. Never draw it as a continuation of the module line.
- **The orbiter docks nose to deep space, bay facing the station** [S15], not nose-first into the station.
- **Lighting:** fluorescent in 2011 [S47]; no coloured LED mood lighting.
- **Clocks:** the crew lives on GMT [S32]; sunrise and sunset every ~45 min do not change the working day.
- **No sound outside**, no flames, no showers, no laundry [S33], no chairs.
- **No artificial gravity.** Nothing rests on a floor by its own weight; everything is strapped, Velcroed or drifting.
- **Do not overcrowd.** Twelve people aboard at most on this date [S15].
- **Suits:** the white US suits are used only for spacewalks [S2]; Russian Sokol suits only inside Soyuz for launch and landing [S2]; no one wears a suit for everyday work.
- **Exercise machines:** in 2011 Zvezda had the TVIS treadmill [S2], not the later BD-2 seen in 2013-2014 timetables [S19, S20].
- **Robonaut 2** is aboard but switched off [S13].
- **Solar wings:** the large US wings are on the truss ends; Zvezda's and Zarya's smaller wings are separate [S2]. Do not mix them up.
