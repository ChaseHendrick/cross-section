# Research dossier: Ford Highland Park plant, 1914 (scene id `factory`)

Prepared for the scene illustrator. World units are metres. Every factual claim carries a source key (S-numbers, full URLs in section 11) or is flagged **[unverified, illustrative]**. Where sources disagree, both are given.

The backbone of this dossier is **S1**, Horace L. Arnold and Fay L. Faurote, *Ford Methods and the Ford Shops* (New York: The Engineering Magazine Co., 1915). It was written inside the plant between February and November 1914, with dated observations, staff counts, line speeds, a scaled plan of the chassis lines, and a February 1914 ground plan and second-floor plan of the works. I read its full text and checked the key numbers against the page scans (page images are cited as `archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/nNNN`; book page numbers are given as "p."). **S2**, the National Historic Landmark nomination (prepared 1977), says which building held what. David Hounshell's *From the American System to Mass Production* is the standard modern account, but its archive.org copy needs a login, so I could not open it and nothing here is cited to it.

## 1. Subject and date

**Subject:** the Ford Motor Company's **Highland Park plant**, Highland Park, Michigan (Woodward Avenue at Manchester Avenue, just north of Detroit), making the **Model T** with the new moving assembly lines. The plant is real and well documented. The **arrangement** in the drawing is an explicitly labelled **composite** (see section 3): real buildings, real storey heights, real floor uses and real machines, set out in one line in the order a Model T was made. They do not stand in one straight row on the actual site.

**Moment:** a weekday in **late autumn 1914**. The nominal date is **Thursday 12 November 1914**. This is a chosen date, not a documented event. Three things make this window the best choice:

- **The chassis lines were mature.** They were chain-driven "high lines" (rails at waist height) from 27 February 1914. On 30 April 1914 the three lines built 1,212 chassis in one 8-hour day, at 1 hour 33 minutes of labour each [S1 p.139, n161].
- **The new six-storey buildings were working.** Ground was broken in July 1913 and they were finished about 20 August 1914 [S1 p.391, n415]. From then on, bodies were painted, upholstered and topped on moving chain lines inside them [S1 p.360, p.384 text].
- **The Five-Dollar Day was in force.** It was announced 5 January 1914 [S5; S2], along with the Sociological Department [S6] and the 8-hour shifts [S1 p.59 to 61 text; S10].

**Named real buildings the drawing is modelled on:**

- **Building H**, the four-storey range along John R Street. The NHL nomination calls it "Factory H", 75 by 760 ft, completed about 1911, and "the site of the first moving automobile assembly line" in late 1913 [S2]. S1 also calls it Building "H" [S1 p.376 to 377 text].
- **The one-storey saw-tooth machine shop** between the older ranges [S2; S1 p.23, n43].
- **The power house** on Woodward Avenue [S1 p.4, n24; S2].
- **The gray-iron foundry** [S1 ch. XIII].
- **The south new building on Manchester Avenue** with its glass-roofed craneway [S1 ch. XV; S2]. The nomination names the 1913 to 1914 pair Factories W and X.

**Why this subject:** this is where the moving assembly line was born [S2; S10]. Few factories of any period have a contemporary, dated, illustrated description as detailed as S1.

## 3. Cutaway composition

### 3.1 The composite and why

The real plant was a grid of long buildings at right angles: four-storey ranges, one-storey saw-tooth shops, glass-roofed craneways and streets. In any single straight section, Building H and its chassis lines would be cut crosswise, and the lines would point straight at the viewer. The drawing therefore uses a **process panorama**. Each building is drawn as a true longitudinal slice, with its main motion running left to right, and the slices are joined in the order a Model T was made.

**Put a small caption on the drawing:** "The works rearranged in the order a Model T was made. Buildings, floors and machines are real; their places on the site are not."

**True geography** (for a site-plan inset or tour text) [S1 plans n45 and n46, p.3 n23, p.388 n412; S2]:

- **Woodward Avenue (west):** the main office and garage at its middle, and the engine house and new power plant (with a round tank) to their north.
- **The old works:** inside Woodward Avenue (west), Manchester Avenue (south) and John R Street (east).
  - Long four-storey ranges ran along Woodward and Manchester and up the west side of John R Street (Building H).
  - A one-storey saw-tooth machine shop filled the middle. Craneway No. 1 ran north-south through it and Craneway No. 2 east-west.
- **East of John R Street** along Manchester: the two new six-storey buildings, joined to the old works by bridges over John R.
- **The foundry** lay to the north, next to the railway. Its east end was about level with the east end of the new buildings [S1 p.387 to 388].
- **The railway:** the Detroit Terminal Railway, a belt line, ran along the north boundary [S1 p.23 n43].

**Viewing side:** the viewer stands on the Manchester Avenue (south) side looking north, so left is west and right is east, as on the real site. The near (south) walls are cut away. Building H, the foundry and the machine shop are rotated in the composite so that their long axes run left to right.

**Suggested extent:**

- **Bounds:** x from -10 to 415, y from -6 to 65, z from 0 to 50.
- **Frame:** x 0 to 404, y -2 to 30.
- **Aspect:** the main body is about 404 m by 28 m (roughly 14:1). The chimneys add height at the left end.
- **Depth:** buildings 18 to 23 m deep. The new building's craneway and north building sit behind the south building (z 18 to 49).

**Vertical datum:** y = 0 is the ground floor of every building and the street. The new building's railway track is sunk 1.07 m below its first floor [S1 p.391 n415]. The power-house basement goes down to about y = -4.5 **[unverified, illustrative]**.

### 3.3 Zone table

**Materials, from the sources, for every building:**

- **Structure:** reinforced concrete frame. Red brick facing at corners and piers, contrasting with grey concrete piers and floor-slab bands [S2].
- **Glass:** the walls of all factory buildings were about 75% glass, double strength, in steel sash [S1 p.24 n44]. Building H's windows had many small panes, giving the nickname "Crystal Palace" [S2; S9]. New-building sash is pivoted top and bottom [S1 p.394 to 395].
- **Roofs:** concrete, tar and gravel. Saw-tooth roofs on the machine shop and foundry. Wired-glass lantern roofs on the craneways [S1 p.24 n44; S2].
- **Interior colour, from S1's photographs:** whitewashed walls and columns with a darker dado band about 1.5 m high (p.389 n413, p.397 n421). Mushroom-capital columns (p.143 n165).

**Colour guide:** concrete `#c9c3b5`; brick `#9a4a36`; steel sash `#3a3d3a` **[sash colour unverified, illustrative]**; whitewash `#ece6d8`; dado `#6c6a62` **[tone from photos, hue illustrative]**.

**Lighting:**

- **Daylight:** through the big windows and saw-tooth lights.
- **New buildings:** about 2,000 ceiling reflectors, each a cluster of four 60-watt tungsten lamps [S1 p.401 n425]. Each craneway was to have fourteen 5,000-candlepower Cooper Hewitt "quartz" lamps in the roof peak, 60 ft apart and 76 ft above the ground [S1 p.401 n425]. Draw them a cold blue-white **[colour of the lamps unverified, illustrative]**.
- **Old shops:** enamelled reflector lamps hanging over the lines (visible in S1 p.143 n165 and p.401 n425).
- **Foundry:** the cupola and the ladles glow.

## 4. People

### 4.1 The workforce in numbers

- **Workforce:** S1 says the company once hired 500 men a day "to maintain a working force of about 15,000" [S1 p.46 n68].
- **Nationalities:** "no less than 53 distinct types of nationalities" (1915) [S4]. S1's list of Highland Park's people (Feb 1914, village population about 20,000) runs:
  - Hungarians, Poles, Serbians, Armenians, Bohemians, Russians, Jews, Romanians, Bulgarians, "Slavs", Italians, Croatians;
  - Swedes, Norwegians, Austrians, Black Americans, Irish, Scots, English, Dutch, French, Germans, Danes, Welsh;
  - Spaniards, Japanese, Greeks and Mexicans [S1 p.22 to 23, n42 to n43].
- **Black workers** were few before the First World War. By its end, 1,675 Black workers were employed at Ford [S8]. Keep them to a handful in 1914.
- **Wages:**
  - The lowest rate in March 1914 was 29 cents an hour [S1 p.60]. The 1915 hiring rate was "from 34 cents upward" [S4].
  - Under the profit-sharing plan, 34 cents an hour plus a 28½-cent profit rate made $5 a day [S3].
  - On 28 March 1914, 57½% of workers had been pronounced "acceptable" for bonus rates [S1 p.61].
  - Women in the factory: 32 cents an hour minimum [S1 p.60].

### 4.2 Roles

The costume notes apply to all roles below:

- **Men:** flat cloth caps (most common), collarless or soft-collared shirts with sleeves rolled, waistcoats, dark work trousers. Long white or tan bib aprons on assembly benches (S1 p.110 n132 photo). Foremen in jackets and ties, some in bowler hats **[foremen's dress illustrative]**.
- **Women:** white shirtwaists (blouses), long dark skirts, hair up (S1 p.401 n425 photo).
- **Safety rules:** no loose or ragged clothing; goggles for grinding [S4]. So no neckties flapping near belts on the shop floor.
- **Skin and hair:** a mix of Central, Eastern and Southern European looks, plus a few Black, Mexican and Japanese workers.

## 5. Animals and small life

**No animal is documented inside the Highland Park shops** in the sources consulted. Keep animal life sparse and flag it.

- **Horses** on Woodward Avenue and John R Street: horse-drawn delivery wagons were still common in Detroit in 1914 **[unverified, illustrative]**. Note the pun in S1: the rear axles were once tested on "small wooden horses" (trestles) before the idlers were laid [S1 p.151 n173]. These are not animals.
- **Sparrows and pigeons** on window ledges and in the craneway roof trusses **[unverified, illustrative]**.
- **A cat** in the warm power-house basement or boiler room, the watchman's friend **[unverified, illustrative]**.
- **Rats and mice:** the 1915 handbook warns employees to cover garbage cans against "flies, dogs, cats and rats" at home [S4]. Nothing documents them in the plant **[illustrative only]**.
- **No pit ponies, no guard dogs** (not documented).

## 7. Environment

  - **Woodward Avenue** (west): broad, with telegraph poles and wires [S1 p.3 n23 photo].
  - **Manchester Avenue** (south): the workmen's entrance [S1 plan n45].

  - **The belt railway** is on the north [S1 p.23].
- **Neighbourhood:** workers' houses, boarding houses and shops nearby **[illustrative]**.
- **Sky and weather for 12 November 1914:** not researched. Draw a typical Michigan late-autumn day: low grey cloud, bare elm trees, puddles on John R, wet pavement reflecting the lit windows **[illustrative]**. Daylight is about 9 h 50 min at this latitude in mid-November, sunrise about 6:20 and sunset about 4:10 local standard time. These times are computed, not sourced, and Detroit's legal time zone in 1914 is **[unverified]**.

## 8. Captions

Format: number, zone, title, text, source, confidence. "Verified" means I opened the source and it states the fact. S1 page links go to the scan page I checked where one is given; otherwise to the full text (`https://archive.org/download/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog_djvu.txt`), cited by book page.

1. **John R Street (42). "Out of the door under its own power."** Each finished chassis was driven out through a door onto John R Street. In early 1914 new cars came out at about 40-second intervals. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n173. **Verified.**
2. **Chassis line (27). "Six feet a minute."** An endless chain pulled each chassis along the line at 72 inches (6 feet) per minute. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n164. **Verified.**
3. **Chassis line (27). "From 728 minutes to 93."** In September 1913 a chassis took 12 hours 28 minutes of labour to build standing still. On 30 April 1914, on three moving lines, it took 1 hour 33 minutes. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n161. **Verified.**
4. **Chassis line (26). "Rope and windlass."** The first moving chassis trial, in the slack August of 1913, dragged chassis along a 250-foot line with a rope and windlass. Six assemblers walked with each one. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n158. **Verified.**
5. **Chassis line (27). "Tall men, short men."** The first high line had rails 26¾ inches above the floor for tall men. Two lines at 24½ inches were for shorter men. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n161. **Verified.**
6. **Engine drop (28). "The motor arrives at station ten."** Chain hoists on overhead tracks lowered each motor onto its frame at the tenth of 45 operations. Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7213/pg7213.txt and https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n162. **Verified.**
7. **Tank platform (27). "One gallon to start."** On the gasoline-tank bridge each tank received one gallon of gasoline before the chassis moved on. Source: S1 p.144 to 145, https://archive.org/download/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog_djvu.txt. **Verified.**
8. **Pit (31). "The man in the pit."** Near the end of the line a workman in a pit beneath the chassis capped the front-axle bracing globe. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n162. **Verified.**
9. **Starter (32). "Starting the motor."** The rear wheels rested on a motor-starting drive. Two men filled the radiator and worked the starting lever before the car was driven away. Source: S1 p.150, full text URL above. **Verified.**
10. **Door D (33). "Forty-five operations."** Chassis assembly was split into 45 operations. At number 45 a driver took the car out onto John R Street. Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7213/pg7213.txt. **Verified.**
11. **Body chute (43). "The body chute."** Bodies slid down a chute from a platform. Men under a rocking gallows frame slung each one and lowered it onto a waiting chassis. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n173. **Verified.**
12. **Body chute (43). "Four men and a body."** Before the chute rig, bodies slid down an incline from the second floor. Four men lifted each one onto its chassis on the pavement. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n161. **Verified.**
13. **Idlers (42). "Testing on the track."** Rollers on ball bearings let the motor drive the rear wheels while the car stood still on the track. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n173. **Verified.**
14. **Magneto line (20). "The first moving line."** About 1 April 1913 the flywheel magneto went onto a sliding line. Twenty-nine men each did one step instead of one man doing it all. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n158 and https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7213/pg7213.txt. **Verified.** S1 p.112 also says "about May 1, 1913".
15. **Magneto line (20). "Forty-four inches a minute."** The chain was tried at 60 inches a minute (too fast), then 18 (too slow), then 44, which stayed. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n162. **Verified.**
16. **Magneto line (20). "Twenty minutes to five."** One man used to need about 20 minutes per magneto. On the moving line it took about 5 minutes of one man's time. Source: S1 p.112 to 115, full text URL above; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7213/pg7213.txt. **Verified.**
17. **Test blocks (23). "Run in by electricity."** Each new engine was turned over by an electric motor on a test block. None passed until it reached 750 revolutions a minute on 20 amperes at 230 volts. Source: S1 p.130, full text URL above. **Verified.**
18. **Test blocks (23). "Signed in steel."** Each tester stamped his own letter on a screw head and painted another screw head red when a motor passed. Source: S1 p.130, full text URL above. **Verified.**
19. **Cylinder blocks (17). "Fifteen blocks at once."** An Ingersoll milling machine milled the underside and bearing seats of 15 engine blocks in one batch. One survives at The Henry Ford. Source: https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/the-henry-fords-ingersoll-milling-machine-and-mass-production-at-highland-park. **Verified.**
20. **Cylinder blocks (17). "Forty-nine holes."** A special machine drilled screw holes in a cylinder casting from four sides at once: 49 holes in one operation. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n25. **Verified.**
21. **Tool room (16). "247 toolmakers."** In February 1914 the tool-making department employed 247 toolmakers, making the jigs, fixtures and special machines. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n26. **Verified.**
22. **Machine shop (16 to 19). "Motors, then belts."** Electric motors drove groups of machines and later single machines, so tools could be placed in working order instead of along long line shafts. Source: https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/the-henry-fords-ingersoll-milling-machine-and-mass-production-at-highland-park. **Verified.**
23. **Power house (2). "5,000 horsepower."** Ford's own 5,000-horsepower gas engine drove the plant. A 1,500-horsepower gas engine turned an 850-kilowatt dynamo and an air compressor. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n24. **Verified.**
24. **New power plant (4). "Gas and steam together."** Ford was building five combined gas-and-steam engines of 6,000 brake horsepower each, with gas pistons 42 inches across and a 72-inch stroke. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n24. **Verified.**
25. **Mould carriers (10). "Moulds on the move."** Moulds rode on shelves hung from overhead chains, passing the pourers at about 12 feet a minute. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n358. **Verified.**
26. **Foundry (10 to 13). "A trade in two days."** Of about 1,450 foundry men, most were specialised labourers. A newcomer was expected to learn his one job in two days. Source: S1 p.327 to 328, full text URL above. **Verified.**
27. **Cupolas (7). "Iron at half past six."** The cupola blast went on about 6:10 a.m. and molten iron began to run at 6:30. Source: S1 p.358, full text URL above. **Verified.**
28. **Cylinder floor (11). "The old way."** Engine-block moulds were still made on the floor and poured from ladles hung from electric cranes. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n358. **Verified.**
29. **Craneway (52). "Raw stuff up, cars down."** Cranes lifted rough stores to the top floors. Work then descended by gravity toward the railway at the bottom. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n412. **Verified.**
30. **Craneway (52). "Two 5-ton cranes."** Each craneway had two 5-ton electric cranes riding 80 feet above the track, with a hook lift of about 75 feet. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n423. **Verified.**
31. **Craneway roof (52). "Lamps in the peak."** Each craneway was to have fourteen 5,000-candlepower Cooper Hewitt lamps in the roof peak, 76 feet above the ground. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n425. **Verified.**
32. **New building (46 to 51). "Hollow columns."** Washed air from roof fans was blown down through the hollow concrete columns to heat and cool every floor. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n413. **Verified.**
33. **Paint line (50). "Painting on the move."** Bodies travelled about 25 feet a minute along an 850-foot painting track that wound over three floors. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n384. **Verified.**
34. **Paint line (50). "Brown, then blue-black."** A masked man sprayed brown metal primer with a giant atomiser. The blue-black second coat was flowed on from nozzles fed by gravity. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n385. **Verified.**
35. **Women's department (49). "Women at the machines."** Women made car tops on the fourth floor of the south building. Their pay ran from 32 cents an hour upward. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n425 and S1 p.398 to 399 text. **Verified.**
36. **Cushion line (49). "134 cushions an hour."** Seat cushions were built on a chain moving about 8 feet a minute. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n395. **Verified.**
37. **Top department (48). "Made to measure."** Each windshield and top was fitted to its own body, one of the few hand-fitted jobs in the plant. Source: https://archive.org/details/fordmethodsandf00faurgoog/page/n392. **Verified.**
38. **Wheel room (36). "Spun dry."** Wheels were dipped in paint and spun at several hundred revolutions a minute to throw off the surplus. Source: S1 p.376 to 377, full text URL above. **Verified.**
39. **Gate and employment office (24). "Five dollars a day."** Announced on 5 January 1914, the plan added a profit share to wages for qualified workers. Within days crowds of job seekers were turned away with fire hoses. Source: https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/fords-five-dollar-day. **Verified.**
40. **Employment office (24). "The investigators."** To earn the bonus, a worker's home could be visited. Investigators checked cleanliness, boarders, school attendance and savings. Source: https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/popular-research-topics/ford-motor-company-sociological-department-english-school. **Verified.**
41. **Classroom (24). "Learn English."** Free English classes met before or after shifts. Pupils repeated words as the teacher held up a kettle or a bar of soap. Source: same as 40. **Verified.**
42. **Time clocks (46). "Known by number."** Every hourly worker wore a numbered German-silver badge and passed the time clock going in and out. Source: S1 p.44, full text URL above. **Verified.**
43. **Shortage chaser (any). "The shortage chaser."** A young man hunted down missing parts all day and chalked each department's output on a blackboard at fixed hours. Source: S1 p.64 to 67, full text URL above. **Verified.**

## 10. Pitfalls

- **No body drop inside the plant in 1914.** Bodies met chassis outside on John R Street: first by four men lifting, then by a chute with a gallows frame, then via the chassis-body assembly bridge [S1 p.139, p.151, p.370]. Ford wrote that "only the chassis was then assembled in the line" [S3].
- **Do not draw the River Rouge plant.** No blast furnaces, coke ovens, ore boats or slips. The final line moved to the Rouge only in 1927 [S2].
- **Do not draw later Highland Park buildings.** Factories Y and Z came after 1915, the long one-storey factories and "tracks" about 1918, Factory AA about 1917, and the Sales and Service Building about 1920 [S2]. In 1914 only W and X were built; S1 says space was left for five more [S1 p.387 to 388].
- **The craneway is not a palace roof.** "Crystal Palace" referred to the walls of glass [S2; S9]. Only the craneways and the auto shed had glass roofs [S1 p.24].
- **The 1914 Model T** [S11]:
  - Black paint only, from 1914.
  - A flat, five-sided hood with no louvres (louvres came in 1915 to 1916).
  - Acetylene headlamps; electric headlamps replaced them during the 1915 to 1916 models. S1's operation list includes "places acetylene-gas pipe" [S1 p.148].
  - Wooden artillery wheels, 30 inch clincher tyres, three pedals.
  - A brass radiator shell in 1914 **[unverified]**.
  - No self-starter **[unverified for 1914; not checked]**.
- **Bodies were not made at Highland Park in 1914.** They were bought from outside makers (S1 counts five separate factories) [S1 p.29]. Painting and trimming moved in after August 1914; body woodworking was only being installed late in 1914 [S1 p.410].
- **No forklifts, no hard hats, no safety glasses as standard, no fluorescent tubes.** Use hand trucks, Ford haulers, the monorail and cranes; caps; goggles at grinders only [S4]; tungsten lamps and mercury-vapour Cooper Hewitt lamps.
- **Machine drive.** Do not drive the whole shop from one steam engine through long line shafts. Highland Park used electric motors driving groups of machines through short shafts and belts, and some machines singly [S7]. Belts and overhead shafting are still right for the tool room and many machine groups (S1 p.6 photo, safety chapter).
- **The rope trial was 250 ft.** It was a single line, not "350 ft" (an OCR misreading in some digitised copies) [S1 p.136 n158; S3].
- **Two dates for the magneto line.** S1 gives both "about April 1, 1913" (p.136) and "about May 1, 1913" (p.112). Ford says "along about April 1, 1913" [S3]. Use "spring 1913" if a caption must be safe.
- **Which building?** The NHL nomination puts the first moving assembly line in Factory H [S2]. S1 sometimes calls the chassis lines part of "the machine shop". Ford calls John R Street "the famous street that runs through our Highland Park factories" [S3]. The orientation of the original 1910 building is reported differently (S9 says "parallel to Woodward"). Keep site-plan claims general.
- **The five-dollar day was not paid to everyone.** It was a profit share for men over 22 with dependants and "approved" conduct, married men, some young men supporting relatives, and women supporting kin [S1 p.58 to 61; S4; S5]. New hires after 23 October 1914 had to wait six months [S4]. Many workers on the floor were still on the base rate.
- **Hours.** In March 1914 most production departments worked two shifts (6:30 to 3:00 and 3:30 to 11:40), not three. Only some trades ran three 8-hour shifts round the clock [S1 p.60]. Ford corporate history speaks of three shifts and a 24-hour plant [S10]; both can be shown by department.
- **Women.** Women are documented in offices, magneto coil work, upholstery and top making. Do not put them on the chassis line (not documented).
- **Later institutions.** No Henry Ford Trade School (founded later; not checked), no Fordson tractors (1917 onward [S8]), no Sociological Department after 1921 [S5].
- **Black workers were few in 1914.** Large numbers came with the war years [S8].
- **Line speed versus spacing.** The documented 6 ft a minute and 300 chassis per line per shift imply less than a car length between chassis (see section 6).
- **Bells, not a factory whistle.** Bells are documented [S1 p.221].
- **Real people.** Do not depict Henry Ford, James Couzens, John R. Lee, Charles Sorensen, Clarence Avery or other real named individuals as characters. They may be named in tour text only for documented acts, such as the 5 January 1914 announcement by Ford and Couzens [S5].
