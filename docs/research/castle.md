# Research dossier: Conwy Castle, North Wales, c. 1300

Scene id: `castle`. Prepared for the procedural cross-section illustrator. World units are metres.

Confidence conventions used throughout:

- **verified**: stated in a source listed here that was actually opened during research.
- **measured**: taken by scaling Cadw's published plan (scale bar 0 to 30 m), accurate to roughly plus or minus 1 to 2 m.
- **[illustrative]**: a reasonable reconstruction or artistic choice that the sources consulted do not confirm. Use it freely in the drawing, but never present it as fact in captions.

## 1. Subject and date

**What we depict.** Conwy Castle (Welsh: Castell Conwy) in Gwynedd, North Wales: a real, specific building, not a composite. The scene is a single working day in **late spring (mid May) around the year 1300**, about thirteen years after the castle was finished. The constable's household and the standing royal garrison are in residence. The royal apartments in the inner ward are kept ready for a royal visit but are mostly empty, which matches what is recorded: the inner ward "could, if necessary, be sealed off ... although in practice [it was] rarely used by the royal family" (Wikipedia, Conwy Castle).

**Optional siege-preparation mode.** A switchable variant set in **the winter of 1294-95**, when Edward I himself was trapped at Conwy by the Welsh revolt of Madog ap Llywelyn. He reached Conwy shortly before Christmas 1294, lost his baggage train in an ambush, the town was burnt, and he was besieged and supplied only by sea until relieved in February 1295 (Wikipedia, Conwy Castle; Wikipedia, Madog ap Llywelyn). See section 4.5.

**Why Conwy.**

1. It is the best documented and best preserved of Edward I's North Wales castles for domestic life: Cadw describes "the most intact set of medieval royal apartments in Wales" and historian Jeremy Ashbee calls them the "best preserved suite of medieval private royal chambers in England and Wales" (Cadw; Wikipedia).
2. It was finished by 1287 (Cadw listing), so in 1300 it is complete and lived in, not a building site. (Caernarfon and Beaumaris were, to the best of general knowledge, still unfinished around 1300; this was not separately checked for this dossier.)
3. Its long, narrow plan on a rock ridge (two wards in a row, eight towers, a barbican at each end) is naturally suited to a single long panoramic cutaway.
4. The original 1283-84 garrison is recorded by trade in the Welsh Rolls (Lewis 1912), which gives us real head-counts.
5. Every key household space in the brief exists here and is documented: great hall, lesser hall, chapel, royal chapel, kitchen, bakehouse ovens, brewhouse, stables, well, prison tower with a pit cell, gates with portcullis and drawbridges, wall walks, garden and water gate.

**Named real example.** Conwy Castle only. Generic household practice (meal times, servants' hours, wall painting) is borrowed from documented English castles of the same decade, chiefly Goodrich Castle (Herefordshire), where Countess Joan de Valence's household accounts of 1296-97 survive (English Heritage). This borrowing is flagged where used.

**Real people are not characters.** Historical names (Edward I, Master James of St George, the constables) may appear in captions only. Every character in the casting list is fictional.

## 3. Cutaway composition

### 3.1 Viewpoint and what is cut

- **Viewer stands south of the castle, over the tidal inlet of the Gyffin stream, looking north.** West (the town gate) is on the left, east (the River Conwy estuary) is on the right. At midday the sun is behind the viewer, lighting the south faces.
- **Front plane (layer F)**: the section plane runs west to east along the south side. It removes the south curtain wall and the southern halves of the SW Tower, Bakehouse Tower and King's Tower, so we see into: the SW Tower (constable's rooms, bread oven), the whole hall range (lesser hall, small chamber, great hall, passage, chapel, cellars), the inner-ward south range (king's hall and king's chamber) and the King's Tower. Draw cut stone faces with a darker hatched "section" tone.
- **Prison Tower exception**: the Prison Tower projects south directly in front of the great hall, so a full section would hide the hall. Draw it as a **sectioned stump**: the pit cell (y -3.5 to 0) shown in section, everything above about y 0.6 removed with a jagged break line, so the great hall reads clearly behind it. Its upper floors can appear in the exploded-slice mode.
- **Courtyard band (layer C)**: use a gentle raised oblique view (about 25 to 30 degrees from above). The courtyards appear as a receding ground band between the front buildings and the back layer. People crossing the wards walk in this band.
- **Back plane (layer B)**: the north side, seen across the courtyards with its courtyard-facing walls cut away: NW Tower, guard rooms, timber kitchen, brewhouse and stables, Kitchen Tower, Stockhouse Tower, granary, the east range, and the Chapel Tower with the royal chapel. **Draw layer B with a vertical offset of about +14 m and a scale of about 0.85** so it rises above the front roofs.
- **King's Tower**: cut it down at its roof platform (y about 14) and omit or ghost its turret, so the royal chapel in the Chapel Tower (behind it in layer B) stays visible.
- **The well**: in plan it sits in the north-east corner of the outer ward, behind the Bakehouse Tower. Use cutaway licence: show the windlass at the well head in layer C, and draw the 28 m shaft in section in the rock below the front plane at x 63 to 67.5. Note the licence in the fact card.
- **Below ground**: cut the rock in section along the whole length so cellars, the prison pit and the well shaft are visible. The rock is grey sandstone and limestone.

### 3.2 Left to right, bottom to top

Left: the edge of the walled town, the steep ramp and drawbridge pit, the west barbican with three turrets. Then the SW Tower and the west gate passage. Then the long hall range filling the outer ward's south side, with the Prison Tower stump in front, and the kitchen, brewhouse and stables visible above and behind. Then the cross-wall with the Bakehouse Tower in front and the Stockhouse Tower behind, the rock-cut ditch and middle drawbridge, the well. Then the inner ward with the royal apartments in front and the granary, east range and Chapel Tower behind. Then the King's Tower, the east barbican garden, the water-gate stairs and dock, and the open estuary on the far right.

Bottom to top: water and foreshore, rock face, cellars and pit, ground floors, first floors, second floors, roofs and wall walks, turrets and banners, sky.

### 3.4 Palette (all [illustrative] except where noted)

- External walls: limewashed off-white, about `#ECE6D6`, streaked grey-green near the base and under the merlons. The castle was painted white with limewash (Cadw teachers' notes, verified).
- Bare stone (inside walls, worn patches): grey `#8C8A83` to brown-grey `#7A7266`.
- Window and door dressings: pink to red sandstone, about `#B5786A` (Cadw listing: "freestone dressings of pink sandstone", verified).
- Plastered interiors of royal and best rooms: white `#F2EEE4` with thin red-ochre lines in a brick-like "masonry pattern" `#A33B2A` (documented practice of the period, the-past.com on Goodrich).
- Timber (oak): `#6B4A2F` inside, weathered silver-grey outside.
- Roof coverings in 1300: not documented in the sources consulted (the roofs were renewed in lead in 1346). Use a neutral dark grey `#5E6266`.
- Rock: `#7D7668` with yellow and grey-green lichen dabs.
- Water: grey-green `#6F8A84`, browner at low tide over mud and sand.

## 4. People

### 4.1 Head-counts

The best record is the establishment set in the Welsh Rolls for 1283-84 (Lewis 1912, page 25): **30 men in total, including 15 crossbowmen (balistarii), 1 attiliator (artiller, keeper and repairer of weapons and armour), 1 chaplain, 1 mason (cementarius), 1 carpenter, 1 smith (faber), and 10 others (residuum)**. Lewis notes the "others" included the keeper of victuals, the doorkeeper (janitor) and watchmen (vigilatores). The constable's yearly fee was £190. Cadw summarises the same garrison as "fifteen crossbowmen and fifteen other servants" (Cadw teachers' notes). Lewis also warns that the North Welsh castles "were seldom so strongly garrisoned" as on paper, so by 1300 numbers may have been lower.

Our scene: **about 50 people inside** = the 30-man royal establishment + the constable's private household (about 10, [illustrative]) + visitors (about 8, [illustrative]).

### 4.2 Roles (31 roles)

Clothing notes apply to c. 1300: men wear a linen shirt and braies, a woollen tunic (cote) to the knee or calf, often a sleeveless surcoat, hose to the knee with garters, pointed shoes; working men tuck the tunic into the belt and wear a white linen coif, a hood with shoulder cape, or a straw hat. Women wear an ankle-length kirtle with a sleeveless surcoat or mantle, a narrow belt, and a veil and wimple, or a linen barbette (chin band) with fillet and hairnet. Common dyes: reds, yellows and greens; a strong blue was fashionable (all from Wikipedia, 1200-1300 in European fashion). Ordinary people wear undyed browns and greys [illustrative].

### 4.5 Optional siege-preparation mode (winter 1294-95)

- **Season**: December to February: short days (sunrise about 08:10, sunset about 15:50 local sun time, [approximate]), cold, rain, river in flood (World History Encyclopedia says the castle "could not be reinforced until the spring because of heavy floods"; Wikipedia says it was supplied only by sea).
- **Who is added**: Edward I and part of his household and army. Numbers are not given in the sources consulted; show tens of extra people [illustrative]. The king's rooms in the inner ward are lit and busy.
- **Visible changes** [illustrative unless noted]: timber hourds (fighting galleries) fixed on beams in the square holes below the battlements (Cadw identifies those holes as hourd beam sockets); barrels of crossbow quarrels broached and issued [illustrative]; crossbowmen doubled on the walls; smoke rising from the burnt town (the town was burnt, Wikipedia, Madog ap Llywelyn); ships unloading grain, fish and meat at the water gate (relief by sea, Wikipedia).
- **Food crisis vignette**: the chronicler Walter of Guisborough says Edward refused to drink his own wine and had it shared with the garrison (Wikipedia). Cadw's guidebook treats the story as unproven (reported by lostfort blog). Label it "a chronicler's story".

## 7. Environment

**Setting.** The castle sits on a rocky coastal ridge (Wikipedia) at the eastern edge of the walled town, overlooking the crossing of the River Conwy (Cadw teachers' notes). The south face rises from rock above the tidal mouth of the Gyffin stream; the east end overlooks the wide estuary. There is **no bridge** in 1300: the crossing is by the royal ferry (Lewis 1912). Across the river are the hills where the old castle of Deganwy stood abandoned (Wikipedia). Inland lie the mountains of Snowdonia.

**Town (left and behind).** A new English colony: 1.3 km of town walls with 21 towers, three gates and a row of twelve latrines (Cadw teachers' notes); a royal watermill outside the Mill Gate; a quay by the Lower Gate (Wikipedia, Conwy town walls). Llywelyn's hall still stands in the town wall in 1300; it was dismantled and moved to Caernarfon in 1316 (Wikipedia, Conwy town walls). The former Cistercian abbey church, left behind when the monks were moved to Maenan in 1283-84, became the parish church (Wikipedia, Aberconwy Abbey).

**Weather and sky (mid May)** [illustrative]: bright spells and fast showers off the Irish Sea, white cumulus over the mountains, wet stone glinting after rain, a rainbow over the estuary after a shower. Wind mostly from the west or south-west.

**Sea state**: a sheltered estuary, small chop when the tide runs against the wind; at low water wide banks of sand and mud with channels; at high water the river fills almost to the rock.

**Ambient sounds** (for optional audio) [illustrative]: gulls and oystercatchers; wind on the battlements and flapping banners; the chapel bell at Mass; the bell of the town church; hammering from the smith; ringing of mason's chisels; crossbow thunk and the thud of bolts into the butt; horses stamping; laundress bats; kitchen clatter and spit-fire crackle; the porter's challenge at the gate; the drawbridge chains at dusk; water slapping at the dock; Welsh and English voices; at night, the watchmen's calls and the tide.

## 10. Pitfalls

1. **No stone arches in the great hall.** The surviving transverse arches were inserted in the mid 14th century (1346-47); in 1300 the roofs stood on timber trusses (Cadw teachers' notes; Cadw listing).
2. **Not grey.** The castle was limewashed white (Cadw). Do not draw bare, ruined grey stone, ivy, grass on wall tops or empty window holes.
3. **No bridges** across the river. The suspension bridge (1826), rail bridge (1848) and road bridge (1958) are modern (Wikipedia). Use the ferry.
4. **The modern visitor path and the rebuilt Bakehouse Tower are not medieval.** The original entrance was the ramp on the north side (Cadw); the Bakehouse Tower was breached in 1655 and rebuilt in the 1870s (Cadw listing).
5. **The conical slate roof on the Chapel Tower is a restoration** (Cadw listing). The 1300 roof forms of towers and turrets are not documented in sources consulted: use simple flat roofs behind parapets.
6. **No gunpowder.** The earliest known European picture of a cannon is in the Milemete treatise of 1326 (Wikipedia). No cannon, no gunports.
7. **No full plate armour.** Around 1300 soldiers wore mail and padded coats, with coats of plates appearing; complete plate suits only by about 1420 (Wikipedia, Plate armour). Crossbowmen in brimmed kettle hats and padded aketons.
8. **The garrison's missile weapon is the crossbow**, per the 1283-84 establishment (Lewis). Do not make them longbowmen.
9. **Heraldry**: three gold lions on red. No fleurs-de-lis (added 1340), no modern Welsh flag (its green and white field is Tudor and the flag was adopted in 1959) (Wikipedia).
10. **No hourglasses or mechanical clocks** in the castle. The first clear evidence of a sandglass is a fresco of 1338 (Wikipedia).
11. **No hops, potatoes, tomatoes, maize, turkeys or tobacco.** Ale is unhopped (Wikipedia, Beer in England); the others are American and post-1492 [general knowledge, not separately sourced].
12. **No 15th-century fashions**: no steeple hennins, no long pointed poulaines, no doublets with puffed sleeves. Use c. 1300 tunics, surcoats, wimples and barbettes (Wikipedia, 1200-1300 fashion).
13. **No uniforms.** There is no evidence in the sources consulted for matching garrison uniforms; vary colours [illustrative judgement].
14. **Not Tudor room names.** "Presence chamber" and "privy chamber" are labels from a survey of 1627 (Cadw plan). Use king's hall and king's chamber.
15. **No "oubliette".** Call it the prison pit or basement cell.
16. **Welsh people did not live in the castle or town**, nominally (Lewis); do not show Welsh families at home in the borough.
17. **No glass everywhere.** Glazing in 1300 is not documented here except that the chapel lancets are glazed today; draw shutters on most windows, with glass only in the royal chapel and king's rooms [illustrative].
18. **Do not set it in 1283-87.** No scaffolding spirals or 1,500 builders in the main scene; the castle is finished.
