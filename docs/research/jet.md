## 1. Subject and date

**Subject:** a **Pan American World Airways (Pan Am) Boeing 747-121** (This Day in Aviation identifies the inaugural aircraft as a Pan Am 747-121 [S5]; the FAA type certificate lists 747-121 serial numbers 19637-19661 and others [S3]) in its original 1970 two-class configuration, flying **Pan Am Flight 102, New York (JFK) to London (Heathrow)**, an overnight eastbound transatlantic service.

**Moment depicted:** a night in **June 1970**, about three hours out of New York, in cruise at 35,000 ft over the North Atlantic. The April 26 to June 30, 1970 Pan Am timetable lists Flight 102 as a daily **747**, leaving New York at **19:00** and arriving London Heathrow at **06:35** local time, class code "FY", which the timetable footnote defines as "President Special - Rainbow Service (First and Economy)" [S4]. New York was then GMT-4 and London GMT+1 in that table [S4], so the scheduled block time is 6 h 35 min **[derived]**. The drawing can cycle from sunset after take-off, through a short night, to dawn before landing.

**Why this subject and date:**
- Pan Am was the 747's launch customer: it ordered 25 747-100s in April 1966, and the 747 entered service with Pan Am on 22 January 1970 on New York to London [S7].
- The 747-100 type certificate was approved on 30 December 1969, for four Pratt and Whitney JT9D engines and a minimum crew of three (pilot, copilot, flight engineer) [S3].
- Boeing still publishes the original airport-planning manual for the 747-100/200/300/SP (D6-58326), with dimensioned drawings of doors, decks, cabin sections, cargo holds, containers and landing gear [S1, S2]. It gives this scene a primary-source skeleton.
- 1970 sits before two later changes that an illustrator could wrongly mix in: Pan Am's upper-deck **dining room** (reported from about 1973 [S36]) and the stretched upper deck with straight stairs (747-300, 1983 [S7, S13]).

**Real examples the drawing is modelled on:**
- The **inaugural service** of 21/22 January 1970: the scheduled aircraft N735PA (named *Clipper Young America*) had engine trouble, and N736PA (*Clipper Victor*) flew the trip, departing at 01:52 EST about seven hours late [S5, S6, S7]. This Day in Aviation gives 332 passengers and 20 crew, a 6 h 14 min flight and 30,000 lb of baggage and cargo [S5]; Northwestern University's 747 anniversary site gives 335 passengers and 20 crew [S25]. Sources disagree about which aircraft carried which name that night [S5, S6].
- **Composite elements (declare on the caption panel):** the flight is a typical June 1970 Flight 102, not a specific dated trip; the aircraft carries no specific registration; every person is fictional; the room positions inside each zone are laid out from Boeing's 1970s "basic mixed-class" plan [S1] adjusted to Pan Am's reported seat counts [S12], and are marked as layout estimates in section 3. Recommendation: do **not** paint a real registration or Clipper name, because N736PA was later lost in the 1977 Tenerife collision [S5]; leave the script-name panel as an unreadable flourish.

**Classes in 1970:** only two. First Class ("President Special") and Economy ("Rainbow") [S4]. Pan Am offered 362 seats: 26 first-class seats in zone A, 32 in zone B, and nine-abreast economy in zones C, D and E [S12]; the upper deck was a first-class cocktail lounge with 16 seats that were not sold [S12].

## 4. People

### 4a. Totals

- **Flight deck:** 3 (captain, first officer, flight engineer): the FAA minimum for the 747-100 [S3].
- **Cabin crew:** about 17. The inaugural flight carried 20 crew in total [S5, S25], which leaves 17 if three were on the flight deck **[derived]**. Rule from the type certificate: one attendant at each No. 3 overwing exit when passengers are carried [S3].
- **Passengers:** up to 362 seats, 58 of them first class [S12], leaving 304 economy **[derived]**; the reported zone split is C 104, D 86, E 114 **(R)**, which sums to 304. The inaugural carried 332 [S5] or 335 [S25]. Use **about 340** for this June night **[unverified, illustrative]**.
- **Upper deck:** lounge seats were not sold [S12]; the type certificate limits upper-deck occupancy on a single-exit upper deck to 8, or 16 with an improved slide and smoke barrier [S3]. For take-off and landing, lounge visitors return to their main-deck seats **[practice inferred, illustrative]**.

### 4b. Roles

Uniform notes: Pan Am's stewardess uniform for the 747 era was "The Uniform for the Superjet Era" by Frank Smith of Evan-Picone, in **Superjet Blue** or **Galaxy Gold**: A-line jumper dress or flared camisole skirt, Chesterfield jacket, bowler hat, white cotton blouse with a detachable stock tie "that could be removed in-flight" [S18]. A March 1969 Pan Am caption describes "the beige jumper, Chesterfield-style coat, and blue skirt with matching jacket" [S15]. ## 6. Machinery and moving parts

### 6a. How the JT9D turbofan works (for the cutaway engine)

The JT9D is a two-shaft high-bypass turbofan [S9]. Air enters the inlet and meets the **fan** (one stage, 46 solid titanium blades [S11], 2.34 m across [S8], each blade carrying a part-span shroud that locks with its neighbours to form a stiffening ring [S11]). Most of the air the fan pushes goes **around** the engine core through the fan duct: the bypass ratio is about **5.1** [S10], so roughly five-sixths of the air never enters the core **[derived]**. The rest passes through a **3-stage low-pressure compressor** (on the same shaft as the fan), an **11-stage high-pressure compressor**, the **annular combustor** where kerosene burns, then a **2-stage high-pressure turbine** (driving the HP compressor) and a **4-stage low-pressure turbine** (driving the fan) [S8, S9, S10]. Overall pressure ratio 22.5 and total airflow 696 kg/s at sea-level take-off for the JT9D-7 [S10].

Ratings: JT9D-3A take-off thrust 43,500 lb dry, or 45,000 lb with water injection (2.5 minute limit); maximum continuous 36,400 lb [S3]. The 747-100 carries 400 US gal of injection water for this [S1]. The 747-100 type certificate allows JT9D-3, -3A and -7 series engines [S3]; which variant each Pan Am aircraft had in June 1970 is **[unverified]**. This Day in Aviation lists JT9D-7A engines for N736PA [S5], probably a later fit **[unverified]**.

Early trouble: casing "ovalization" under take-off loads made high-pressure turbine blade tips rub; it was cured with a stiffer casing and yoke-shaped thrust links, and engine problems left about thirty aircraft parked outside the factory during testing [S8].

## 7. Environment

- **Altitude and speed:** cruise at 35,000 ft, Mach 0.84, 893 km/h [S5]. At 35,000 ft (10,668 m) the NASA standard-atmosphere model gives about **-54 C** outside [S29]; the speed of sound there is about 297 m/s, so Mach 0.84 is about 249 m/s true airspeed **[derived]**. Great-circle JFK to Heathrow is about 5,540 km (2,990 nautical miles) **[derived]**.
- **Inside:** cabin pressure altitude no more than 8,000 ft under normal conditions [S30]; warm, dry air **[illustrative]**.
- **Sky cycle:** depart in evening daylight; sunset behind the tail; short summer night with a pale glow on the northern horizon at these latitudes in June; dawn ahead from about 03:30 GMT; sunrise over the cloud sea before descent **[derived from the timetable and general astronomy, illustrative]**.
- **Below:** a broken to solid cloud deck several kilometres below, with occasional gaps showing a dark sea; moonlit cloud tops if the moon is up **[unverified, illustrative]**. At these heights the sky above is deep blue-black with sharp stars.
- **Weather:** usually smooth air with occasional light turbulence near jet-stream boundaries; seat-belt sign flicks on, drinks ripple **[illustrative]**.
- **Ambient sounds:** steady roar of the engines with a high fan whine (see 6b); hiss of air from the gasper nozzles; the two-tone PA chime **[illustrative]**; clink of glasses and ice in the lounge; cart wheels and rattling bottles; oven timers in the galleys; film soundtrack heard only as a faint tinny buzz from neighbours' tube headsets; muffled lavatory flush; a baby crying, then quiet; the flight engineer's pencil; HF radio static on the flight deck **[all illustrative]**.

## 9. Hidden details and stories

1. **The stock tie comes off.** A stewardess unclips the stock tie at her neck once the seat-belt sign is off. *Documented feature of the uniform* [S18].
2. **Junior wings.** A stewardess pins plastic or metal junior wings on a child's jumper. *Documented airline custom* [S28]; that it happens on this flight is plausible invention.
3. **Eggs to order.** At dawn the first-class galley steward cracks fresh eggs into a pan. *Documented Pan Am claim* (undated) [S21].
4. **The lounge has no tickets.** A guest finishing his Scotch is politely sent downstairs to his own seat before descent. *Lounge seats not sold: documented* [S12]; *the upper-deck occupancy limit: documented* [S3]; the scene itself: plausible invention.
5. **The roof hatch.** Above the flight engineer's head, a hatch with coiled escape reels. *Documented* [S7].
6. **Silent laughter.** A row of passengers wearing tube headsets laugh at a film nobody else can hear. *Documented technology* [S20, S22].
7. **Smoke in the projector beam.** A blue haze from cigarettes shows the beam of light to the screen. *Smoking documented* [S27]; projector-beam detail is plausible invention.
8. **The fuel log.** The flight engineer writes columns of numbers every half hour by the light of a small lamp. *Plausible practice, unverified.*
9. **A dog in the bulk hold.** A kennel with a sleepy spaniel among suitcases. *Plausible invention.*
10. **The spiral photographer.** A passenger leans over the spiral stair rail with a camera. *Plausible invention*; the stair is documented [S1, S14].
11. **Seasick ashore and aloft.** A pale passenger clutches a paper sick bag. *Plausible invention.*
12. **Empty water tank.** The injection-water tank, used up at take-off, sits empty in the belly. *Tank and 2.5-minute use documented* [S1, S3]; position illustrative.
13. **Cardboard Clipper postcard.** A honeymooner writes "the stairs!" on a 747 postcard. *Postcard slogan documented* [S25].
14. **The overwing guard.** A stewardess dozes upright on her jump seat beside door 3. *Requirement documented* [S3]; dozing is invention (and should be shown only during the quiet hours, not at take-off).
15. **The model 747.** A businessman in zone B carries a gift-shop 747 model for his son. *Plausible invention.*

## 10. Pitfalls (avoid these)

- **Wrong upper deck.** The 747-100 has a short hump with three windows each side and a **spiral** stair [S1, S13]. The long upper deck with straight stairs and many windows is the 747-300 (1983) or a later -100B/-200 option [S7].
- **Dining room too early.** Pan Am's upper-deck dining room is reported from about 1973 [S36] (a 1977 annual report shows it [S16]). In June 1970 it is a cocktail lounge [S12].
- **No business class.** The 1970 timetable shows only First (President Special) and Economy (Rainbow) [S4].
- **No glass cockpit, no two-crew flight deck.** Analogue dials and a flight engineer [S3, S7].
- **No winglets.** Those belong to the 747-400 (1989) [S7].
- **Right engines.** JT9D only; GE CF6 and Rolls-Royce RB211 came on later 747 variants [S1, S7].
- **No seat-back screens or modern headphones.** Projected films and pneumatic tube headsets [S20, S22, S23]. CRT video projectors came later [S22].
- **No lower-deck galley or galley lift on a Pan Am 747 in this picture.** Boeing offered it as an option and American Airlines used it [S1, S24]; do not mix it into Pan Am.
- **No piano.** The Wurlitzer piano bar was American Airlines' coach lounge, not Pan Am [S24].
- **Not ten-abreast.** Pan Am economy in 1970 was nine-abreast [S5, S12].
- **Livery.** Black, widely spaced "PAN AM" titles and the blue globe, 1970 to 1973. The later bold "Pan Am" billboard titles and the post-1973 lettering are anachronistic [S26].
- **Uniforms.** No reversible cape or "Tally Ho" Borsalino hat (1971-75 update) [S18]; no trousers for stewardesses in this picture **[unverified, illustrative]**.
- **No nose cargo door.** Only freighter and convertible versions have a hinged nose [S1].
- **Smoking is normal.** Ashtrays in armrests, cigarettes in the lounge [S27]. Do not draw no-smoking placards everywhere.
- **No mobile phones, laptops, wheeled cabin bags or digital watches.** **[general period knowledge, illustrative]**
- **Cargo doors are on the right.** The forward, aft and bulk cargo doors are right-side only [S1]; from the port-side view they are on the far wall.
- **APU off in cruise; landing gear up; flaps in.** **[general operating knowledge, illustrative]**
- **Don't name the aircraft after the Tenerife loss.** N736PA *Clipper Victor* was destroyed in 1977 [S5]; leave the registration off.
