# Research dossier: Balao-class fleet submarine USS *Pampanito* (SS-383), night of 11 to 12 March 1945 (scene id `submarine`)

Prepared for the scene illustrator. World units are metres. x is measured from the bow tip (x = 0) towards the stern (x = 95.0); y is height above the bottom of the keel (y = 0). Every claim carries a source key (S-numbers, full URLs in section 11) or a flag:

- **[unverified, illustrative]**: plausible, not confirmed by a source I opened. Draw it if useful, never caption it as fact.
- **[layout estimate]**: a position read off a builder's plan or a period drawing and scaled, not a dimension printed on the plan.
- **[derived]**: arithmetic from printed figures (for example frame spacing converted to metres).

Primary basis: the boat's own *General Information Book* compiled by Portsmouth Navy Yard in 1943 for SS-381 to SS-404 (S1), which prints Pampanito's lengths, heights, frame spacing, machinery, complement and a compartment-and-tank plan (Plate 6(A)). It is supplemented by the museum's room-by-room tour (S2 to S23), the 1946 Navy training manual *The Fleet Type Submarine*, NAVPERS 16160 (S29 to S40), the Navy's 1940s medical study of 1,471 war patrol reports (S41) and the ship's orders of a sister fleet boat, USS *Pompon* (S43).

Tone note: this is a scene of ordinary night routine (charging batteries, baking bread, reading mail). It should not depict attacks, sinkings or casualties. Pampanito's war record included the sinking of a ship carrying Allied prisoners of war in September 1944 and the rescue of 73 survivors (S44, S28). That history deserves respect and is deliberately not the subject of this scene.

## 1. Subject and date

**Subject:** USS *Pampanito* (SS-383), a *Balao*-class diesel-electric fleet submarine built by Portsmouth Navy Yard, Kittery, Maine: laid down 15 March 1943, launched 12 July 1943, commissioned 6 November 1943 (S44). She made six war patrols in 1944 and 1945 (S27, S44), was decommissioned at Mare Island on 15 December 1945, became a museum at San Francisco in 1975, opened to the public on 15 March 1982 and is a National Historic Landmark (S44, S28). The drawing is **modelled on Pampanito herself**, not a composite class drawing, wherever her own documents give the answer.

**Default moment: Sunday night, 11 to 12 March 1945, during Pampanito's sixth war patrol, on the surface at night charging batteries, off the east coast of the Malay Peninsula (South China Sea, southern approaches to the Gulf of Siam).**

- The sixth patrol ran from the afternoon of 25 February 1945 (departing Subic Bay) to 24 April 1945 (arrival Pearl Harbor), operating "off the Malaysian Peninsula" in a wolfpack with *Sealion* and *Mingo*; the island of Pulau Redang is named in the patrol narrative (S24, S44).
- On 11 March 1945 she met *Pintado* and *Sea Robin*; *Sea Robin* "had 34 sacks of long overdue mail for delivery". The museum's history adds that "Christmas had finally caught up with Pampanito", though some of the cookies were moldy (S24). Whether the mail was passed by day or night is not stated; the scene simply assumes the mail is aboard that night.
- On her return the captain and crew were congratulated on "a hard and boring patrol" (S24). This makes the patrol an honest, non-combat setting.
- 11 March 1945 was a Sunday [derived from the calendar]. New moon fell on about 14 March 1945, so the night of the 11th to 12th had only a thin waning crescent rising shortly before dawn: an essentially moonless night [derived from the mean lunar cycle; approximate].
- The exact position that night is not given in any source I opened; place the boat generically off the Malay coast [unverified, illustrative].

**Optional "dive" moment (same night):** a rehearsed or precautionary dive, using the documented procedure: two blasts on the diving alarm, engines stopped, shift to battery, vents opened, periscope depth in about 30 seconds (S33, S22). The boat then surfaces again with three blasts (S33). No enemy needs to be shown.

**Why Pampanito, and not *Cod* or *Bowfin*:**
- Pampanito's builder's book (S1) survives in the museum's online archive with her exact lengths, frame spacing, heights of decks, bridge, periscopes and guns, machinery ratings, battery data and designed complement, plus a dimensioned compartment plan. No other preserved boat offered that precision to me.
- She "escaped the guppying process" after the war and "retains her original design and fittings" (S28).
- The San Francisco Maritime National Park Association publishes a room-by-room tour (S2) and a patrol-by-patrol history (S24 to S26).
- USS *Cod* (Cleveland) is a superb alternative, unique in having no visitor doors cut into her pressure hull (S50), but she is a *Gato* with General Motors engines. USS *Bowfin* (Pearl Harbor, open since 1981) is a *Balao* (S51) but I found no comparable builder's book for her.

**Configuration in March 1945 (important, it changed in mid-1945):**
- Main deck gun: **a 4-inch gun forward of the conning tower**, "which she had carried forward of the conning tower on all six patrols" (S24). The 1943 book lists a 4"/50 Mark 12 mount (S1 p.26).
- After the patrol, at Hunters Point in June to July 1945, the 4-inch was replaced by a 5-inch gun aft, twin 40 mm guns went on the gun decks, a twin 20 mm went on the forward main deck, and an SV radar and sonar dome were fitted (S24). **None of these belong in a March 1945 drawing.**
- Anti-aircraft guns in March 1945: the 1943 book lists two 20 mm mounts at frames 48 and 59, the forward and after ends of the bridge (S1 p.21, p.26). The museum tour describes a photo with a single 20 mm on the forward gun deck and a 40 mm on the after gun deck (S22). Which applied in March 1945 is **[unverified]**; recommended: single 20 mm forward, single 40 mm aft, matching the tour photo.
- Radar: SJ surface-search radar (S8, S48) and SD air-search radar masts behind the periscopes, the original Portsmouth arrangement (S45).
- The museum interprets the boat as she was in "late summer 1945" (S27). The drawing deliberately shows her five months earlier.

**Composite elements to declare on the caption panel:** room positions are measured from the 1943 builder's plan, which may differ in detail from 1945. Night routine, meals and lighting come from Navy manuals and sister-boat documents (S34, S41, S43), not from a Pampanito log for that night. Every clickable person is fictional. The real commanding officer and crew are not portrayed.

### 2d. Hull outline key points (for the side silhouette)

- **Deck line:** (0, about 8.3) at the stem top; (1.4, 8.27) at the forward perpendicular; sloping gently down to (36.8, 6.99) amidships; then nearly level to (95.0, 6.80) at the stern [S1, derived; the shape between these points is a layout estimate from Plate 6(A)].
- **Bow:** a raked, convex curve from the stem top down and back to the keel, reaching the flat keel at about x = 12 to 14 [layout estimate from Plate 6(A)]. The upper bow is the free-flooding bow buoyancy tank (S22, S63).
- **Keel:** flat at y = 0 from about x = 13 to x = 72; the bottom then sweeps upward aft, reaching about y = 2 at the after end of the pressure hull (x = 87.5) [layout estimate].
- **Stern:** the hull narrows to a rounded tail at about y = 4.5 to 6.8 at x = 95 [layout estimate]. Beneath it hang the rudder (rudder area 98.22 sq ft = 9.1 m², S1 p.21) and the horizontal stern planes.
- **Propellers:** two, one each side; right-handed to starboard, left-handed to port; solid four-bladed, diameter 7.791 ft (2.37 m), design pitch 8.812 ft (2.69 m); lower blade tip 2 ft 11 1/4 in (0.90 m) above the keel, so the hub centre is about y = 2.1 (S1 p.24). Shaft centreline is 2.06 m above base and 1.73 m off the centreline at frame 128 (x = 89.4) (S1 p.25), sloping down aft at 1 degree 30 minutes (S1 p.21). Place the propeller discs at about x = 90 [layout estimate].
- **Cross-section:** a round pressure hull about 4.9 m across, wrapped amidships by bulging outer saddle tanks that give the 8.3 m beam at y = 3.7 (S1, S46). The outer hull merges with the pressure hull at the torpedo rooms, so the ends are single-hulled (S46). Above the pressure hull sits a narrower, free-flooding superstructure carrying the deck (S46).

## 3. Cutaway composition

**Viewpoint:** a single long panorama of the boat in profile, **bow to the left**, so the viewer looks at her **port side**. The near (port) half of the outer hull, ballast tanks and pressure hull is cut away along the centreline, in the style of the Navy's own 1946 cutaway (S35). Equipment that actually stands on the port side (the diving station, the "Christmas tree" panel, the radio room, the wardroom; S4, S7) should be drawn standing just behind the cut line in the foreground, so it is not lost. Starboard fittings sit against the far wall. If the engine wants bow-right, mirror with x' = 95.0 - x.

**What is visible, bottom to top:**
1. Dark sea below the keel (keep 10 to 12 m of water for the optional dive).
2. The outer hull and external tanks (ballast, fuel, trim) as a thin band under and around the pressure hull, shown in section with a few tank labels.
3. Lower level (y = 1.1 to 3.5): battery wells, pump room, magazine and cold stores, lower engine flats, motor room, lower torpedo rooms.
4. Main level (y = 3.5 to 6.0): every living and working space, end to end.
5. The waterline cutting across the hull at y = 4.6: the upper third of the hull, the deck and the whole bridge are above water at night on the surface.
6. Superstructure, deck, conning tower, bridge, periscope shears and masts (y = 6 to 20).
7. Night sky.

**Suggested frame:** world x from -8 to 104, y from -12 to 24, an aspect ratio of about 3.1:1. For a wider strip, crop y to -6 to 22 (4:1). The sea surface sits about one third of the way up the frame. Keep the raised periscopes (to y = 20.3) inside the frame.

**Light:** almost everything outside is black-blue. Inside, at night, the Navy practice was red light in the control room, conning tower and living spaces so men going topside kept their night vision (S34, S41). On USS *Grayback* red lighting was used "in the crew's space, maneuvering room, control room, conning tower, forward battery, and the forward and after torpedo rooms at night", with "GE-Ruby 25-watt" bulbs (S41). That list leaves out the engine rooms and the galley, so a strong pattern for the picture is: **red compartments fore and aft, bright white light in the galley and engine rooms** [Grayback practice applied to Pampanito; illustrative]. The bridge is completely dark (S43).

## 4. People

### 4a. Totals (verified)

- Designed complement in the 1943 builder's book: 7 officers and 69 enlisted, total 76 (S1 p.27).
- Wartime: "10 officers, 70 to 71 enlisted" (S44, S45); the museum tour speaks of 80 men (S11, S17).
- Navy medical study: the average complement on war patrols was about 75 enlisted men and 8 officers; average age about 26.7 for officers and about 22 for enlisted men (S41, S42).
- **Use 80 people aboard: 9 officers and 71 enlisted.** No women served aboard US submarines in 1945.
- About a third of the crew is on watch at any moment: the crew "operated in three watch sections" (S11).
- In 1945 the Navy was still segregated; it was formally desegregated by an order of 27 February 1946 (S53). Before the war almost all Black enlisted men had been restricted to the steward's branch (S53). The two steward's mates in the complement (S1) should be drawn as Black or Filipino sailors, shown with the same dignity and detail as everyone else.

### 4b. Roles

Clothing baseline: "At sea there is no prescribed uniform. Individuals are expected to be guided by their own common sense, pride in personal cleanliness, and a sense of decency" (USS *Pompon*'s orders, S43). The Navy working uniform of the time was dungarees: a blue chambray shirt, white T-shirt and denim bell-bottom trousers with the name stencilled on them (S52). Khaki was authorized aboard submarines from 1931 and as the officers' working uniform on all ships from 1941 (S52). In the heat of a long dive men "stripped down to shorts" and took off shoes and socks (S41); sandals are mentioned (S41). Bridge watchstanders used rain trousers and parka jackets in foul weather (S41). Night lookouts wore red goggles before going up (S32, S34). ### 4c. Daily schedule (a patrol day, ship's time, composite)

Pampanito's 1944 pattern was to stay "submerged during daylight" and travel "on the surface at night to run the diesel engines to charge the batteries" (S25). Near 5 degrees North in mid-March, night lasts about 12 hours [derived; ship's clock zone unverified].

Watch periods: the traditional four-hour watches are 2000 to 0000 (first), 0000 to 0400 (middle), 0400 to 0800 (morning), 0800 to 1200 (forenoon), 1200 to 1600 (afternoon), with dog watches 1600 to 1800 and 1800 to 2000 (S56). In a three-section boat each man stood about 8 hours of watch a day (S41: "regular 8 hours of watch"). Whether Pampanito dogged the evening watch is **[unverified]**. Heavy-weather bridge watches could be shortened to 3 or 2 hours (S41).

## 5. Animals and small life

- **No documented Pampanito mascot** was found in the sources I opened. Do not show a named Pampanito pet as fact.
- Fleet-wide practice: "Mascots were commonly carried aboard the boats: dogs, a cat now and then, occasionally a monkey" (S41, lightly quoted). Documented dogs include "Luau" on *Spadefish* and "Betty" on *Whale* (S41). An optional small dog may be added as a labelled **plausible invention**.
- **Cockroaches:** "A grim battle was waged throughout the patrol between the crew and the cockroaches" on one boat; "some had rats" (S41). Cockroaches near the galley are well grounded; a rat is possible but less typical.
- **Flies:** a potato store in a forward torpedo room "proved a breeding place for flies" (S41).
- **Outside:** flying fish and phosphorescence in the wake are plausible in tropical seas **[unverified, illustrative]**.

## 7. Environment

- **Place:** South China Sea off the east coast of the Malay Peninsula, near Pulau Redang (S24). Tropical; far offshore in the drawing, with no land visible [illustrative].
- **Season and weather:** March is northeast monsoon season. Later in the patrol, from 28 March, Pampanito met "heavy seas and high northeast winds" (S24). For 11 March use a moderate northeast breeze and a long swell, scattered low cloud, warm humid air [unverified, illustrative].
- **Sky:** moonless (new moon about 14 March 1945, derived), so stars and faint cloud edges only; the Milky Way and the Southern Cross low in the south are plausible from 5 degrees North [illustrative].
- **Sea:** black water with white bow wave and wake; faint phosphorescence [illustrative]. Waterline at y = 4.6 on the hull.
- **The boat herself:** completely darkened (S43), so the only outside light is starlight on wet steel and the wake.
- **Sounds (for optional audio):** steady roar of the diesels (so loud in the engine rooms that men used hand signals, S17); gurgling wet exhaust at the stern; hiss of the bow wave; ventilation and battery blowers; the click of the sound-powered phone and its growl; low voices on the bridge; two blasts of the diving alarm and "Dive, dive" for the optional dive (S33); rushing air from the vents; then sudden quiet as the diesels stop and the boat runs on battery; creaks and the whine of motors and reduction gears, which "produced the loudest underwater sounds" (S19).
- **Smell (for captions only):** diesel and lubricating oil; doctors noticed "the peculiar oily odor" on crews' clothing and skin after a patrol (S41).

## 8. Captions

Each caption: zone, text, source URL, confidence.

1. **Whole boat.** USS *Pampanito*: 311 ft 8 in (95.0 m) long and 27 ft 3 1/2 in (8.3 m) wide, by her builder's own book. Source: https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/ss383-general-info.pdf. Verified.
2. **Whole boat.** About 80 men lived aboard. On war patrols the average officer was about 27 years old and the average sailor about 22. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cm.php?pano=nr and https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/duff.pdf. Verified.
3. **Whole boat.** This was Pampanito's sixth war patrol, from 25 February to 24 April 1945. On her return it was called "a hard and boring patrol." Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/pamphist/patrol6.php. Verified.
4. **Crew's berthing.** On 11 March 1945 another submarine handed over 34 sacks of overdue mail. Christmas had finally caught up with Pampanito, though some of the cookies were moldy. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/pamphist/patrol6.php. Verified.
5. **Exterior.** Two shades of haze grey on the sides blend into the horizon haze; black on top hides the boat from aircraft when she is under water. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/adeck.php?pano=nr. Verified.
6. **Exterior, engine room.** Diesel-electric drive: the engines never turned the propellers. They drove generators, and the electricity charged the batteries, ran the motors, or both. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/aer.php?pano=nr and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balao-class_submarine. Verified.
7. **Bridge.** No snorkel: to run her diesels and charge her batteries she had to surface, usually at night. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/pamphist/patrol2.php. Verified.
8. **Forward torpedo room.** About 14 men slept here alongside 16 torpedoes: six in the tubes and ten reloads. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/ftr.php?pano=nr. Verified.
9. **Forward torpedo room.** A Mark 14 torpedo was 20 ft 6 in long and weighed about 3,000 lb. Reloads were moved by hand with block and tackle. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_14_torpedo and http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/atr.php?pano=nr. Verified.
10. **Forward escape trunk.** A small chamber in the overhead could be flooded and drained so the crew could leave a disabled submarine. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/ftr.php?pano=nr. Verified.
11. **Wardroom.** Officers' dining room, office, chart room and meeting room in one. It held the chronometers, the charts and a record player. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fbc.php?pano=nr and http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fbc-wardroom.php?pano=nr. Verified.
12. **Pantry.** The pantry made only coffee and toast. Officers ate the same meal as the crew, cooked in the same tiny galley. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fbc.php?pano=nr and http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cm-galley.php. Verified.
13. **Captain's stateroom.** The only private room aboard. A depth gauge and compass repeater at the foot of the bunk let the captain check the boat without getting up. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fbc-captain.php?pano=nr and http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fbc.php?pano=nr. Verified.
14. **Forward battery.** Under the officers' feet: 126 battery cells, each about 4 1/2 ft tall and weighing about 1,650 lb. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/doc/fleetsub/elect/chap5.php and https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/ss383-general-info.pdf. Verified.
15. **After battery.** Charging cells give off hydrogen. A sister boat's orders said it must never exceed 3 per cent in the ducts, and put the smoking lamp out when the charge reached its finishing rate. Source: https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/suborders.pdf. Verified (USS *Pompon*, a sister fleet boat).
16. **Control room.** The diving station: one wheel for the bow planes, one for the stern planes. The stern planes hold the angle, the bow planes control depth. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cr.php?pano=nr. Verified.
17. **Control room.** The "Christmas tree" shows a red or green light for every hull opening. A "green board" means all are shut and she can dive. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cr.php?pano=nr and http://legacy.maritime.org/doc/fleetsub/chap18.php. Verified.
18. **Control room.** Two blasts of the diving alarm: in about 30 seconds she is at periscope depth, and at 65 ft in about 35. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/doc/fleetsub/chap18.php, http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fdeck.php?pano=nr, http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cr.php?pano=nr. Verified.
19. **Pump room.** Below the control room: two 3,000 lb air compressors, trim and drain pumps, hydraulic pumps and the air conditioning plant. Nobody stood watch here unless something needed fixing. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/pump.php?pano=nr. Verified.
20. **Radio room.** A radioman was always on watch when the boat was on or near the surface. Coded messages were read on an ECM Mark II cipher machine. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/radio.php?pano=nr. Verified.
21. **Conning tower.** A steel cylinder 8 ft across and 17 ft long: the captain's battle station, where up to ten men could work. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/ct.php?pano=nr. Verified.
22. **Conning tower.** The Torpedo Data Computer, an analog machine of about 1,500 lb, worked out where to aim. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/ct.php?pano=nr. Verified.
23. **Periscopes.** The larger forward periscope was for night use; the slim after one, harder to see, for attacks. Raised, their tops stood about 66 ft above the keel. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/ct.php?pano=nr and https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/ss383-general-info.pdf. Verified.
24. **Radar mast.** The SJ radar used 10 cm waves to find ships on the surface, even on a black night. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SJ_radar. Verified.
25. **Bridge.** Lookouts stood on platforms either side of the periscope shears, each searching his own slice of sea and sky. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fdeck.php?pano=nr and http://legacy.maritime.org/doc/fleetsub/chap20.php. Verified.
26. **Control room ladder.** Before a night watch, lookouts wore red dark-adaptation goggles for at least 20 minutes. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/doc/fleetsub/chap20.php. Verified.
27. **Bridge.** "This ship will run completely darkened at night." No smoking topside when darkened. Source: https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/suborders.pdf. Verified (USS *Pompon*'s orders).
28. **Deck gun.** Pampanito carried a 4-inch deck gun forward of the conning tower on all six war patrols. A 5-inch gun aft replaced it in mid-1945. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/pamphist/patrol6.php. Verified.
29. **Main induction.** One 36-inch valve aft of the bridge drew in all the air for the engines and for breathing. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/adeck.php?pano=nr. Verified.
30. **Galley.** Square pots on rectangular hot plates, because square pots hold more. The bakers worked overnight, and a special pie locker held pie for the whole crew. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cm-galley.php. Verified.
31. **Crew's mess.** Four tables fed the crew in sittings. The watch section going on duty ate first. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cm-mess.php and http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cm.php?pano=nr. Verified.
32. **Cold stores.** Food for a 60 to 90 day patrol filled the freezer and cold rooms, then the shower stalls, the spaces behind the engines and even the deck, under cardboard. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/cm.php?pano=nr. Verified.
33. **Crew's berthing.** 36 bunks for many more men: three men shared two bunks, sleeping in turn. This was called hot bunking. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/abc.php?pano=nr. Verified.
34. **Crew's berthing.** An ice cream freezer, perhaps "the only luxury on the whole boat." Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/abc.php?pano=nr. Verified.
35. **Washroom.** About 70 men shared two toilets, two showers and one washing machine. Fresh water was so precious that a man might shower once every ten days. Sources: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/abc-head.php?pano=nr and http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/fer.php?pano=nr. Verified.
36. **Engine rooms.** Four Fairbanks-Morse diesels, each 1,600 horsepower at 720 rpm, with two pistons in every cylinder working towards each other. Sources: https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/ss383-general-info.pdf and http://legacy.maritime.org/doc/fleetsub/diesel/chap3.php. Verified.
37. **Engine rooms.** On the surface the engines were so loud that the men spoke in hand signals. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/aer.php?pano=nr. Verified.
38. **Maneuvering room.** Every change of speed was ordered from the bridge, conning tower or control room, and set here by electricians at the control stand. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/man.php?pano=nr. Verified.
39. **Motor room.** Four electric motors, two to each shaft, geared down to turn the propellers at up to 280 rpm. The gears were the boat's loudest noise under water. Sources: https://legacy.maritime.org/doc/pdf/ss383-general-info.pdf and http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/mot.php?pano=nr. Verified.
40. **After torpedo room.** Four tubes, four reloads on skids, and bunks for 12 men, with three more that could be rigged. Source: http://legacy.maritime.org/tour/atr.php?pano=nr. Verified.

## 9. Hidden details and stories

1. **The Christmas mail in March.** Men reading letters in their bunks and a tin of moldy Christmas cookies being inspected in the mess. *Documented for Pampanito, 11 March 1945* (S24).
2. **The night baker.** A baker kneading dough under the galley light while the rest of the boat is red and dark; loaves going into the bread locker on the starboard side. *Documented practice on Pampanito* (S12); baking at night on other boats (S41).
3. **The ice cream freezer.** A sailor sneaking a scoop in the crew's berthing. *Freezer documented* (S14); sneaking is *plausible invention*.
4. **Red goggles.** A lookout sitting in the control room with red goggles on, waiting out his 20 minutes before climbing the ladder. *Documented practice* (S34, S32).
5. **Smoking lamp out.** An electrician in the after battery holding an unlit cigarette because the charge has reached its finishing rate. *Documented rule on USS Pompon* (S43); on Pampanito, *plausible*.
6. **Potatoes in the shower.** A sack of potatoes or crates of cans stowed in a shower stall. *Documented for Pampanito* (S11).
7. **The cockroach war.** A cook swatting a cockroach behind the hot plates. *Documented on some boats* (S41); on Pampanito, *plausible*.
8. **Seasick newcomer.** A young sailor green-faced in his bunk with a bucket. Seasickness was common, especially in men new to a boat (S41). *Documented practice*; individual is *invention*.
9. **Movie in the torpedo room.** Off-watch men watching a 16 mm film projected onto a sheet between the torpedo racks. Movies were a major morale item and one boat "showed them in alternate torpedo rooms" (S41). *Documented fleet practice; whether Pampanito had a projector on this patrol is unverified.*
10. **Acey-deucey.** Two sailors hunched over a board in the mess, a ring of watchers. *Documented for Pampanito* (S11); the game was a Navy favourite (S54).
11. **The captain's gauges.** The captain half asleep, one eye on the depth gauge and compass repeater at the foot of his bunk. *Documented fittings* (S6).
12. **One book at a time.** The pharmacist's mate handing out library books from a locker. On *Pompon* the pharmacist's mate was librarian and "only one book may be drawn at a time" (S43). *Documented on a sister boat; plausible on Pampanito.*
13. **Garbage over the side.** Weighted sacks of garbage passed up to the bridge after dark and dropped over. *Documented routine* (S34, S43).
14. **Laundry day.** A man feeding dungarees into the washing machine, whose water partly comes from air-conditioner drip pans. *Documented fittings* (S15).
15. **The cricket.** An electrician's pet cricket in a matchbox. *Pure invention, label it as such*; real mascots were usually dogs (S41).

## 10. Pitfalls

- **No snorkel.** US fleet boats of 1945 could not run diesels submerged (S25). Do not draw a snorkel mast.
- **Not a GUPPY.** No streamlined post-war sail, no bulbous bow, no extra batteries; Pampanito escaped conversion (S28).
- **Wrong gun.** In March 1945: one 4-inch gun **forward** of the conning tower. The 5-inch gun aft, twin 40 mm guns, twin 20 mm on deck and SV radar came only in June to July 1945 (S24). Museum photos of the 5-inch gun show the later fit.
- **Engines do not drive the propellers.** No shafts from the diesels to the propellers; the engines drive generators (S17, S45). Shafts start at the motor room.
- **Four motors, two per shaft, with reduction gears** (S19). Later *Balaos* had slow direct-drive motors; Pampanito did not (S45).
- **Darkened ship.** No navigation lights, no open hatches glowing white, no smoking on the bridge at night (S43).
- **No "uniform" at sea.** Avoid dress blues or whites; working dungarees, khaki, T-shirts and shorts (S43, S52, S41).
- **No visitor doors.** Museum boats (except *Cod*) have access doors cut in the hull (S50). Draw only the original hatches.
- **Watertight doors are small.** The builder's book lists watertight doors of 30 by 38 inches (S1, table of contents). Their rounded shape and raised sill are [unverified, illustrative]; men stoop and step through rather than walk.
- **The hull is round.** Every interior space is a slice of a 4.9 m cylinder: curved overheads, sloping sides, the deck set below the centreline.
- **Hydrogen, not smoke.** Battery charging gives off invisible hydrogen; never draw fumes from the battery wells.
- **Not all compartments are lit red.** Red light was for spaces where men needed night vision; the documented *Grayback* list does not include the engine rooms or the galley (S41).
- **Paint.** Two-tone haze grey sides with black horizontal surfaces (S21), not all-black and not blue camouflage.
- **No kill flags or battle flags flying at sea.** Avoid glorification; the scene is routine.
- **The real crew is not portrayed.** All clickable people are fictional; do not use real names from Pampanito's history.
- **Not *Down Periscope*.** Pampanito played the fictional USS *Stingray* in a 1996 comedy (S44). Ignore that film's set dressing.
- **Test depth.** 400 ft (S44); the tour gives a design test depth of 412 ft and a crush estimate around 600 ft (S7, S3). Do not caption deeper figures as normal operation.
